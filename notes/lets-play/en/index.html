When Learning Becomes a Game

Out of XP, levels, and badges grows a second teaching system – until the question about the other side shrinks it all back down again.

01

GPT Safaris as a Detour

It's August 3rd and I'm working on my math lesson prep.

Yesterday I was on one of my GPT safaris and at some point stumbled onto the topic of game-based learning. And so a hint turned into a question. That was followed by a second question, and so on, until I decided to build the topic into my teaching.

It's a spontaneous decision with an experimental character, and I'm only doing it because I have AI available. I've never done anything like this before and I have no idea how the kids will react to it.

On top of all the lesson drafts and planning, there's now a playful element added to the mix. I give GPT a first, still pretty rough task and see what comes of it.

You:
Create a teaching unit for me on: reflection across an axis, point reflection, axis and point symmetry, and a chapter on application. Focus: game-based & gamified learning. Students age 13, level B.

It creates an entire teaching unit of 4–5 lessons just on the topic of game-based learning. That wasn't my intention, but it's my mistake: I wrote "teaching unit" and "focus: game-based…" in the prompt. I don't intend to do game-based learning for almost a whole week; I want to spread this element across the lessons and use it as a possible wrap-up at the end of the unit.

I realize I need to learn to prompt more precisely, and I need to try to anticipate what might come out the other end of the LLM black box.

For me, the task was clear.
For AI too – just different.

I'm curious and I carry a basically positive attitude. So it's not a problem that this feels like a workbench rather than a finished method. The plan for the teaching unit is already built and consists of the parts topic, goals, methodology, structure, and so on. Now it builds me thematically fitting content for game-based learning – GBL for short – in no time. I run into various terms and learn that game-based learning means learning integrated into a game's storyline. Alongside that is gamification, where I add game elements like points, badges, levels, or feedback loops to regular teaching. For my experiment right now, it's mainly the second one that's relevant: not a finished learning game, but a small learning booster in math class.

I notice that GPT only ever delivers a plan, never a ready-to-copy template. For example, it now gives me a "symmetry hunt," where the teacher projects everyday images (animals, logos, emojis, buildings) onto the screen. Cool idea, but only half as valuable if it still means I have to organize a lot of material myself.

I also notice the varying quality of the answers. Sometimes it's good, sometimes it's bad. Is that my prompts' fault?

I find the conversations with GPT during the thinking and development process very productive. But when it's supposed to deliver something finished – a handout, a worksheet, a piece of material – it gets worse. Is that because I'm only using the free version?

I keep going, and at some point I notice the dominance of English terms.
I actually have nothing against English terms, but I do wonder why it doesn't use German terms. Are there none, or is it just easier for GPT to pull English words from the training pool?
After a few turns, I have my setup together:
On offer, I have a ten-minute gamified warm-up with "Symmetry Hunt."
A foundational lesson on axis reflection with a game: "Mirror Race"
A lesson on point reflection with a digital task
A lesson on axis and point symmetry with a "Symmetry Battle" (quiz game)
An application lesson as a challenge quest on art and nature, as well as games and technology

I read through this list and I have to admit I don't fully understand everything yet. "Challenge quest"? No idea. But I did understand that for every puzzle solved correctly, teams collect points, stickers, gems, and can reach levels like bronze, silver, gold, and diamond. I say teams because I want to use it as a group or class system, not as a public ranking of individual students.
Everything sounds somehow like a new video game, but on the other hand, I have a new class. Something new won't stand out too much there, and this kind of cooperative teaching format fits the situation too. I check the materials list, and with 5 items it seems manageable. Still, I don't trust this list: "Worksheets with mirror puzzles (codes, figures, logos, grid points)"? "Token/XP table on the wall for motivation"? That sounds like hidden work. The problem isn't the work itself, it's that you have to go hunting for what the work actually is. I'm not bad with Word and Excel, but what good is that if I first have to go find the right graphics? I don't want to complain, but searching and finding things eats up time. And I wonder whether GPT couldn't eat up exactly that time-sink for me. It really should.

02

The system becomes concrete

The longer I chat with AI, the more it feels like being in a virtual toy or craft store. Google and books drift far away, which is actually a shame, but in this artificial world there's just too much interesting stuff to marvel at.
There's another difference between Google and GPT, too: I talk to him!

I'm talking to a machine?
Is that even possible, and is it wise?

For a moment it unsettles me what's actually happening between us: are we communicating, even though I'm writing to a machine? I call GPT "him" and "you" and notice that I'm projecting something onto the machine. Then the question fades again, because the pull toward new knowledge is stronger. I already have more questions. I can get rid of my questions, follow-up questions, and the follow-ups to the follow-ups right away, unorganized and direct.

You:
How exactly does this work with the levels, badges, medals?

GPT shows me a level and badge system for my teaching unit: 10 XP for small tasks, 50 XP for difficult challenges or group work, plus +5 XP for team spirit. Whether that actually adds up, I don't check in this moment, but it looks good and that's enough for me right now. Only later will I ask what in all this actually supports learning and what just produces points, titles, and motion. I notice how strongly GPT prioritizes pair and group work. For cooperative learning formats, GPT builds in a team-spirit bonus and awards extra points for it (+5 XP). Does it do that for everyone who asks about GBL? Either way, the points immediately create a visible ladder: with every task solved, students climb up the XP scale.

03

Badges as recognition for special achievements

While I'm working on the badges, I remember someone who once asked me why I prepare my lessons with GPT, what "it" — the AI — can do that a human can't, and whether AI doesn't produce a lot of errors.
I look at the list of suggested badges and have to laugh.

The idea of handing out these badges as small cards, stickers, or digital icons strikes me as creative, and I admit it's something I wouldn't have come up with myself. I don't feel inferior, and I don't feel threatened by something artificial being better at this than I am. Quite the opposite:
"nice move," a smile, and gratitude for AI's output. The further thinking, developing, and deciding I'll do myself.

04

On gold medals that aren't meant for individuals

At the end of the unit, the XP and badges get added up, resulting in medals (not for individuals, but for teams or the whole class). But the XP system is slowly growing over my head, and I want to wrap up the topic. Yet another thought comes to mind:

You:
And the whole thing as an escape game?

Suddenly the experiment no longer has points and levels, but a beginning, four stations, and an end. It looks like an actual lesson, and I build it into a complete teaching unit as an escape game. Will this alternative turn out to be more workable?

The topic stays "reflections & symmetries" – except now the kids have to solve, step by step, four puzzle stations called "The Mirror Code," "The Twin in the Mirror," "The Symmetry Guardian," and "The Mirror Portal" to escape a "mirror labyrinth." Will escaping the labyrinth actually show whether the kids understood reflections across an axis or a point? Or is something else maybe what matters in this lesson design?

With that, my experiment shifts from gamification to game-based learning: the point system steps back and the game narrative moves to the center.

In the second week of school, I'll use the escape game once in math class on the topic of congruence transformations. I'm dropping the XP system — it's getting to be too much. Alongside the math, it would open up a second system I'd have to keep running in parallel. For now, this will stay a single GBL attempt. Lesson idea, teaching tools, YAML, the task library, and the growing system already claim my attention — and the prep effort for GBL is too big to keep pursuing the topic right away.

05

Is this still learning, or just conditioning?

Back to August 3rd: it's gotten to be evening and I'm almost done. Actually, I am done. The handout is finished, but there's that inner teacher voice saying, "Why don't you add just one more…!", "Wouldn't it help to also…?" I push it aside and turn to another aspect I ran into right at the start: the scientific literature. What does it say, and what does it not say? What will the AI say if I afterward let it take on a shift in perspective in satellite mode?

What are the advantages, what's the bright side, and what are the negative aspects and pitfalls? I find it interesting to use the AI for a confrontational conversation, so I ask it a provocative question:

You:
Isn't this gamification stuff basically reward conditioning and external motivation?

What should I call this kind of prompting? Questioning without reflection? Biased and manipulative framing on my part? It's a way of looking at something — not just a left-right switch of sides, but a switch of position between observer and object. I call it "asking GPT about the other side."

This way I create new spaces, new angles, and I go looking for friction, conflict, dissonance — not for the sake of dissonance, but to test my own view from the other side. It's not about truth. I don't want to declare my subjective view the truth. But I do want to widen it by placing other perspectives next to it. In this space I can sharpen a thought without dragging a real person into a rhetorical fight. And I decide the time, the place, and the limits of the fight. That doesn't really work in real life: at a training course, for instance, this kind of argument is limited — I picture the closing round, everyone wanting to go home, me still having a question and raising my hand. And with a book, I can't have an immediate conversation either. With GPT I can flip a thought around in the same moment, ask a follow-up, and push again. I can print out its "tips and tricks" and lay them out on my kitchen table. But I can also turn them over, fillet them, dissect them, analyze them — and then just let them sit, to see, by how they start to smell, how stable a method or an idea still is after a few days.

I could buy thousands of books on "Teaching Successfully," on "10 Super Teaching Methods," and look through their tips and tricks. But they don't show me what GPT can show me: the other side.

I ask GPT for literature and get more names and sources than I can read in one day. I don't take them as hard proof, just as breadcrumbs for later. Over several rounds, I develop an understanding of what game-based learning might actually be aiming at — but at the same time, Deci and Ryan show up in the chat, with autonomy, competence, and relatedness. What started out as just a hunch on my part thickens in the chat into something like a premonition: Is it not the reward itself that carries the learning, but feedback, orientation, and the experience of self-efficacy?

Only through this deliberately provoked contradiction do I see my design more clearly: it's not the points and badges that belong at the center. The game system, large at first, shrinks because of this, and what's left isn't a parallel lesson structure, but simply a learning booster.

These shifts in perspective open up horizons,
not just forward, but in every direction.

And so my understanding of the term "gamification" changes. For me it's now a "learning adventure." I'm no scientist and I'm not claiming what's right and what's wrong. But I now have a preliminary toolbox of terms, questions, and traces for my further path. And I'm aware that these are not yet vetted sources, nor didactically validated answers.

06

What remains is the learning booster

No finished model emerged. More like a working draft V1.0: enough order to tell reward, feedback, and motivation apart, but nowhere near enough to turn into a theory.

I don't want to treat game-based learning as a separate module of teaching, but build it into my lesson ideas as a possible learning booster and as a didactic bridge.

For me, only these questions remain:
Where does it live? Where does it become noticeable in everyday teaching?
Probably not as its own chapter, but as a small workshop technique that will show up wherever it fits.

GBL can't make a good lesson.
But it can make a good lesson a little bit better.
No more, but no less either.

And there's one thing I take with me from the conversations with GPT:
Whenever someone comes to me in the future trying to sell something as a "game changer" or "best practice" in education, I now know:

They never asked AI about the other side.
