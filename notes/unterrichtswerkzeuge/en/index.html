From the Teaching Idea to the Tools

What ended as a concept is now taking shape: six elements emerge. Five of them make it into the classroom.

01

AI-generated tools

It's Saturday, August 23, 2025. School has been back in session for a week. Three weeks ago, I actually just wanted to create a worksheet for the geography topic "Our Earth." Since then, it has turned into an unfinished mosaic of six teaching elements: starter package, learning-goal cards, diagnostic tests, portfolio, reflection, and learning map.
I sketched these elements only roughly for myself over summer break. Not based on any guideline or model, just on my own experience and a dash of curiosity. Now I want to develop and expand these into AI tools.

Working on teaching elements can get very creative, so first I need to get an overview of the latest version:

Over summer break I set myself the goal of having all materials prepared and copied by the first day of school. I didn't manage it. Not because I was on vacation — quite the opposite, I worked every day until late at night on many different projects, and one project completely threw me off track. Namely, the idea of having AI automatically generate worksheets or entire teaching units through some code or program.

It was last Thursday, a week ago, when out of pure curiosity I uploaded a pseudo-YAML file as a prompt to GPT for the first time. And ChatGPT actually generated a worksheet in Word format. This (for me) mega-discovery completely changed how I work with AI. Except it was 96 hours before school started.

I'm definitely no pro when it comes to prompts and YAML, but here's how I put it together today: first I write the task into the prompt window. The YAML file comes along with it and is my notebook — my personal library. I have several of these files by now. For example, I have a file on task formats. It's really a library, structured as a list, clear, expandable, and saved as a file on my PC. I don't have to rewrite the list of task formats in GPT every time — I can just upload this library and say what should be done with it.
I look at the screen and go over the overview with "starter sheet," "learning goals," "diagnostic test" once more. Each single element would be a big project on its own. I don't want to and can't decide for or against any one of them. I want all of them, so I decide to put the elements in a time sequence and build each one as a form individually. Later I want to enrich these with content from my teaching and test them in class. And GPT is supposed to help me with that.

You:
Go ahead and create a "starter package" for TU1.

02

Starter package

I want a proposal for an overview sheet in A4 format for the geography topic. It should contain the topic, learning goals, short tests, portfolio, reflection, and learning map. The starter sheet bundles these elements onto one page. It's meant to give an overview of the topic, be visualized with symbols and icons, and use clear, simple language — a sheet you can hand out to the students at the start. I don't expect a print-ready template, because I know my taste in layout and formatting differs from what the AI proposes.

For working through the elements I have a checklist I go through point by point. The starter sheet is meant to orient and inform; I don't ask more of this first tool than that. Then I move on.

03

Learning-goal cards

They're item two on my list and are supposed to replace the classic work plans — that's my idea, anyway. I want to build these as A5 index cards. Generating learning goals with GPT is something I already know, and it's done quickly; plus, I learned from the last learning-goal battle and think ahead about how I write the prompt.
Still, I can't help myself and abandon my rule of 5 learning goals. Instead of creating one learning-goal card with five learning goals for the entire teaching topic, I have it create five learning goals for the basic requirements and five for the extended requirements. That gives four cards of five learning goals each. I'm still not sure how smart that was of me, and especially how practical it'll be in the classroom.
My idea is that a student could say: "I want to work on the basic requirements." Then they take the "basic requirements" learning-goal card and use it as a combined task/learning-goal sheet. My intention is pedagogically tempting to me: enable choice, take participation seriously, make self-efficacy tangible. But my inner voice of experience warns me: "That's not how school works."

A few days later it will turn out that the skepticism was justified. Offering learning-goal cards is not the same as them being used. Or put differently: just because the students can take something doesn't mean they want to. It's not a setback, but a reminder that my wishes and intentions don't always match the reality the students create. What was missing was a clear sense of commitment. I didn't forget it, I ignored it. I'll have to adjust the concept in October. Maybe less as a card idea and more as a learning-goal matrix that provides clearer, more structured guidance.

04

Short tests

One of my teaching materials offers what are called entry and exit tests. I use these regularly and call them short tests. Their function is diagnostic: find out where you stand, practice, test again. They're the counterpart to the learning-goal cards.
In school, a topic normally takes three to four weeks and ends with an exam. But I prefer the loop: learning-goal card → practice → short test → if something isn't solid, back to the learning-goal card → practice and test again. And so on. Always checking what's solid and where it's sticking.
These are working tools, meant to be used during class. Test – practice – test – practice. These are ideas from Black and Wiliam's formative assessment: check, use feedback, keep practicing, check again. I like Wiliam. He has some very informative YouTube videos.
After a while, the short tests are done. GPT designed them in a traditional way: 5 tasks worth 4 points each. On the back is a simplified self-assessment, inspired by Marzano's learning-progress scales: "I can do this," "I'm still practicing," "I need help." I pick three levels instead of the whole model, which would be too much for my classroom.

Students can solve the mini-test on the front side, correct it, and enter the results on the back. For a brief moment, I imagine the kids someday solving these tests in GPT. GPT as a tutor, creating tasks, evaluating them, and presenting a learning recommendation. I'm not there yet.

05

Portfolio

This element is something new for me. I've never created a learning portfolio before. I do regularly build product-based work for portfolios into my teaching, but those have always come from a textbook.
And today I'm building my own tool. Over many rounds, an assignment sheet for portfolio work turns into a new tool for my teaching. I want students to explore and dig into the content from the unit through discovery and inquiry. They should be able to choose from the topics or combine them themselves, depending on interest and learning style.
For this, I want to create an assignment sheet with a clear structure that gives students freedom of choice, self-responsibility, and motivation through bonus options. At the same time, a simple assessment scheme should let them judge their own product.
The structure follows a classic required-bonus model: five required tasks (P1–P5) and four bonus tasks (B1–B4), for example "Discovery Assignment," "Research Folder," or "Creative Assignment."
GPT's output is good, because the model clearly understands required-vs-bonus and cleanly separates the task categories. A few language tweaks, layout and formatting, and it's done.

06

Reflection

It's Tuesday evening, and I don't really want to build the next piece. But I've finished the "Portfolio" element and still have 50 minutes to wait for my train. So I figure I can manage this "small" topic too. These days I often hear in the education world, "We do reflection in our teaching." Then I ask myself what that's supposed to mean. It bothers me when I get the impression that the topic is only used as a calling card for "outward presentation." "Why does reflection matter specifically for my class?" That's what I ask myself when I take reflection seriously. And right now it's an inner voice. It's telling me it's time. Reflection deserves more weight in my teaching. Not as an add-on, not as show-of-force pedagogy, but as a connected element within a larger system. The question is: how do I do that? And I remember a chat from two weeks ago where I talked with GPT across several turns about reflection. That unstructured conversation started with "reflection and its abuse by education policy," moved on to "its necessity," and ended with "its purposes" and the question "What does the term even mean?"

And that's where I am now: the term feels incomplete and, to me, too backward-looking. It should be just as forward-looking. What good is today's knowledge to me if I can't carry it into tomorrow? A forward-looking reflection. The question: what do I take with me, what will I try differently with the next topic? The paper reflection sheet is just the medium here; whether it's a Post-it or the fridge door doesn't matter. The take-away is the actual goal. I need something for the middle of the learning path and something for the end. But what does "end" even mean here? Isn't the end of one learning path also, at the same time, the middle between two learning paths?

My train's departure is getting closer, and I quickly have both worksheets generated as a first draft. Really nothing spectacular, but it's the most important version there is: version 1.0.

I don't print out the two parts anymore, and I have to smile: "Two reflection sheets for one teaching unit? And why two? Because one has to lead to the other."

I'm not done with this topic, but I have to go.

I close the laptop and walk to the station.

07

Learning Map

The next morning I have three free periods. Time to develop a learning map with GPT. I'm not building the learning map as a standalone element, but as a visualization aid. Visually clear and well-structured, so the kids can see the learning path themselves and try to steer their own path. I take on air traffic control here. GPT doesn't give me a finished learning map as a downloadable graphic file, just an overview. I'm disappointed. I wanted a real map with graphic elements, images, visualizations, and ideally something animated in it too. Instead, even after several turns, GPT only delivers bland tables, unusable graphic sketches; no real map with topic fields and arrows that students can actually read. I'll fold this information into my scheme — but I'll never use it.

Of six elements, five survive.
The learning map doesn't make it into the classroom as a standalone tool. But I distribute its functions: the start sheet takes over the overview, and the learning-goal cards and the reflection sheets together support self-direction.
