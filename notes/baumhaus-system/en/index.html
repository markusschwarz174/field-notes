Field Note · English

Six Pieces, One House

Scattered teaching ideas are supposed to become a whole — but I wonder whether assembling them is enough, or whether something more exists in between.

01

house that isn't one yet

It's August 21, Thursday of the first school week. I'm still carrying around my ideas on gamification and game-based learning, and I'm not sure whether they're shaping my teaching concept too strongly. What actually influences my teaching, anyway? Is it didactic models and concepts from the literature? Or is it simply the pragmatic handling of prescribed teaching material and the constant attempt to adapt my teaching to time, environment, and people?

Three weeks ago, I used AI to design materials for my teaching. They're teaching building blocks like the "starter sheet," the "learning goal card," the "short test," the "learning project," the "review," or the "learning map." These are elements you'll find in classrooms all over the world. I hadn't asked myself whether and how they're connected — until just now. I wonder whether I can mentally arrange the elements into a building, and I try to picture the whole thing as a teaching architecture.

I play around with this image for a while, and it helps me arrange my ideas spatially. But it doesn't explain to me how learning paths move through it. What functions do the individual elements take on along this path? Are they connected? And do I even need to prescribe a fixed path for my teaching at all? I spend the evening with Mistral, searching for answers. At some point I stop and don't pursue this train of thought any further for now.

Two days later, though, I start again. Not with the house, but with its still-unordered parts. I don't have anything left to prepare today, and I'm not bored, but the elements I've created are still lying around unsorted on my desk, somehow. I want to take a closer look at them. Just as they are. Is there an order? A structure? Do they resemble something that already exists? I want to explore this with the help of AI.

Over the past few weeks I've tried Gemini, Mistral, Claude, and Perplexity — not systematically, but with individual prompts: first impression, then move on. I get stuck on MISTRAL. I copy the last compilation from GPT into MISTRAL, write a simple prompt, and hit ENTER.

In the uploaded compilation, Mistral doesn't just see individual materials, but a modular system, and generates an overview table for it.

I look at the overview, and in that moment something shifts without my noticing: I'm no longer looking at individual tools, but starting to search for their architecture.

02

start with teaching concepts and end up…

I ask MISTRAL for further elements that might fit my overview. Its suggestions are "SOLO" and "UDL." At first, neither term means anything to me, and only after asking do I learn that "SOLO" is a taxonomy and "UDL" stands for Universal Design for Learning.

But these teaching concepts don't interest me right now. What interests me is the interplay between my teaching building blocks. I try to steer MISTRAL back to that by writing a new prompt. But my thoughts are no longer with the building blocks — they're with an experience from my own teaching.

You:
In developing lessons, you always focus on individual influences — for example, Hilbert Meyer's ten quality criteria, Bloom's taxonomy, Lohmann's advice, Hattie's effect sizes. In doing so, you forget the interplay. The interaction of the different forces. However scientifically sound a lesson design may be, it can still fail for psychological, social, or emotional reasons.

Mistral answers:

Mistral: Your observation is absolutely central — you're touching on a fundamental problem in teaching research: the fragmentation of didactics into individual models, while actual learning success depends on their interplay.

Mistral then develops this hierarchy:

Mistral: [LEARNING SUCCESS] ↑ [Psychological Safety & Motivation] ↔ [Social Interaction] ↑ [Cognitive Activation] ↔ [Structure & Clarity] ↑ [Subject-Matter Accuracy & Methods]

MISTRAL puts learning success right at the top. That's exactly where I get stuck.

03

… at the meaning of learning

I step away from the day-to-day business of "preparing lessons" and my thoughts ride the elevator up a floor.

You:
In the end, the top goal is learning success. Is that right? Or is there another one? If learning success is at the top, then everything else has to be below it. Column-shaped, pyramid-shaped, inverted pyramid-shaped. How do you see it? Will this change in the future?

I know many forms of teaching in the classroom. What I often experience is that learning success doesn't depend on a single factor, but emerges from a complex interplay. I'm interested in how this interplay works. MISTRAL suggests a "pyramid of teaching quality" to me, placing learning success at the top and subject-matter accuracy as the base. Hmm, I think. Trying to represent a complex system hierarchically doesn't quite match my expectations.

It's gotten late, and something else has long since grown out of this laying-out of teaching elements. I remember my own training and all the different subjects — from developmental psychology to subject-specific didactics. Everything was somehow isolated. Why do we treat subject expertise, classroom climate, and self-regulation separately when they constantly interact in the classroom? What happens if I put these elements in relation to each other? If I say, for example: cognitive activation can run into a void when basic needs aren't met. Or put differently: do Hattie's effect sizes only take effect once Maslow is satisfied?

I keep playing with the idea and pit the criteria against each other: can Meyer's criterion "clear structuring" smother a creativity program that Hattie shows to be effective? I notice how I'm bringing together things that actually come from different models. I take these concepts and models, break them down into their Lego pieces, and put them back together the way I see fit.

The chat keeps drifting further away from the original question toward something higher up: psychological safety and social factors. And I ask myself: how do these influence cognitive possibilities? Am I connecting my subject-didactic thinking closely enough with my pedagogical-psychological thinking?

I ask a different question: will teaching optimized according to Hattie fail if students don't feel safe? — I find this question fascinating.

It's one of those questions I've carried around with me for decades. And now I can ask an AI these questions and get an answer. And in moments like this, the disclaimer simply doesn't matter. "AI can make mistakes. Check important information."

04

Six is too many

A few days later I switch to GPT. Why, I don't even know at this moment. In my teaching I've prepared math and geography with the help of AI. But I haven't yet created all the materials according to the new concept.

You:
Also, if I have to create 6 building blocks for every topic, that overwhelms not just me but the students too. That many tools is great, sure, but it can also be confusing. I don't want linear teaching, but something like what's on pages 1 and 2 of the document. I'd like your analysis of the current structure and then a proposal for optimizing it.

Have I already created the learning projects and quizzes for math? What about geography? What state are the materials in? Are they drafts, usable next-stage versions, or already copy-ready final versions? The problem isn't the number of building blocks, but that the materials for them are at different stages of development.

This is the moment I lose track of everything.

I try to bring order into things with GPT by uploading all my materials. But it doesn't look at the state of my materials first. GPT evaluates the structure and the gamification elements first, and singles out the learning project, review, and quizzes as strengths. On the other hand, there's redundancy: the start sheet and the learning map overlap, as do the learning-goal cards and quizzes. At the same time, it's missing an opening hook and a prior-knowledge check. — I have that on the start sheet, which it very likely never looked at — but that still doesn't make the number of elements any smaller. It proposes a new structure with five flexible main building blocks instead of 6+X:

Again it ends up being five modules – just as with the tools before, the learning map loses its place. This time, though, it's not about the number, but about what holds the modules together. The set becomes more manageable – but the structure is still linear. The chat has gotten long. I close it and start a new one.

05

One structure for everything?

You:
No, what I want is this: the table shows a structure of a lesson sequence, or rather a teaching concept. I'm still having some trouble with it. GOAL A: the teaching structure on page 3 should be state-of-the-art and future-ready. Goal B: I want to create master prompts for the individual modules, so I can later use them to generate modules depending on subject and topic. Can you help me with that?

GPT translates my two goals into four requirements. Much of it sounds like a catalog of modern key concepts:

ChatGPT: is didactically state-of-the-art (AI integration, adaptive learning paths, gamification, VR/AR, collaborative learning, future skills) stays future-oriented (self-directed, personalized learning, sustainability, global collaboration, lifelong learning perspective) is compatible with many subjects and topics stays open to media and technology (analog and digital)

I look at the four points and consider whether I can apply them across subjects: I teach several subjects and am always looking for structures they have in common. Backward compatibility also matters to me. New modules don't necessarily have to replace the old materials. Good worksheets, tasks, and tests that already exist should, as much as possible, remain usable.

It's the same idea I first tried four days before school started, when I uploaded a pseudo-YAML: one prompt, many results. I've known about the idea of master prompts for two weeks now. I build a text instruction for a task in Word and upload it to GPT or MISTRAL. And now this concept is growing: I no longer want a specialized prompt for every single product, but a system built from these reusable master prompts. So one for each module, containing variables for subject, topic, and level. This way, even this tool is slowly turning into an architecture.

06

From modules to thematic worlds

The six individual elements – start sheet, learning-goal card, short test, learning project, review, and learning map – still sit side by side. I don't want to look for more elements, but for their connection. To do that, I first clarify what function lies behind each module: orienting, exploring, creating, sharing, or reflecting.

GPT picks up the idea and develops several thematic worlds from it. It offers me three variants: a neutral one, an adventure world, and a maker/game world. Do my terms fit these images GPT created?

ChatGPT: TEACHING IDEA Start sheet · Learning-goal card · Short test · Learning project · Review · Learning map ↓ BUNDLE FUNCTIONS Orient · Explore · Create · Share · Reflect ↓ CHECK THEMATIC WORLDS neutral: Starting point · Explore · Create · Share · Mirror Adventure: Harbor · Expedition · Workshop · Marketplace · Campfire Maker/Game: Spawn point · Quest · Crafting · Showcase · XP review ↓ PRELIMINARY CHOICE Adventure: Harbor · Expedition · Workshop · Marketplace · Campfire

The neutral variant stays factual, maker/game feels too shaped by gaming language for me. The adventure world appeals to me most: harbor, expedition, workshop, marketplace, and campfire. This gives the five functions a shared image for the first time, but to me the terms still stand in a row.

07

One line isn't enough

I'm not satisfied with the five-part structure I've just developed. Before pouring it into another table, I want to know whether we can think about this more fundamentally. So I press further:

You:
No, I want to know from you what else you would build into a forward-looking, visionary, revolutionary teaching structure. Could this teaching structure even be mapped in two or three dimensions? Would that make sense?

GPT responds with seven suggestions:

ChatGPT: Adaptive learning paths Real-life learning / community impact Immersive experiences Transdisciplinary learning clusters Real-time self-efficacy feedback Open learning time and learning spaces Meta-skills / future skills
That evening, only two of them actually help me: adaptive learning paths and open learning time. The rest sound modern but don't solve my concrete structural problem.

These suggestions don't form another layer above the five modules. They run across the entire structure: adaptive learning paths, real-life learning, or future skills can be effective in several modules at once. So GPT suggests a multidimensional representation:

ChatGPT: Axis 1: Learning phase Axis 2: Learning mode Axis 3: Competency development or difficulty level

Multidimensionality keeps coming up in my teaching development, and I know that approaches to modular and non-linear learning environments have been discussed for a long time. What's new for me, then, isn't the basic idea, but the attempt to represent non-linearity spatially with the help of AI: I no longer want to see it as a sequence, but as a matrix, a map, or maybe even a three-dimensional model.

That's why it bothers me at first that GPT immediately labels the first axis with familiar school terms again: intro, development, deepening, review, and presentation. That's exactly the linear language I want to get away from. Am I thinking too radically?

You:
These traditional terms bother me — 'intro,' 'development,' and 'consolidating results.' Couldn't we replace them with something that breaks away from this linear approach?

But GPT doesn't respond with a suggestion for a spatial representation — it responds with a new vocabulary: DOCK, EXPLORE, CREATE, SHARE, REFLECT, and UPGRADE.
I don't notice that the chat is changing direction:

I asked for a structure and got vocabulary.

08

The English chain doesn't fit

GPT builds the sequence from that and stresses that every step is optional and repeatable, and that learners choose their own path.

Dock → Explore → Create → Share → Reflect → Upgrade

I look at the table, and it still strikes me as linear. The words sound more modern, but again they're lined up as a chain, one after another. I'm looking for a two- or three-dimensional representation — a navigation logic with different starting and end points, doors, bridges, and gaps.

The English terms don't solve my problem. DOCK, EXPLORE, and CREATE don't fit my model linguistically. Instead of pursuing the spatial structure further, I go back to the German adventure-world I'd already chosen. It's not the structure that occupies me now, but the naming again. Even though German terms already exist, I ask again:

You:
What terms would we use if they had to be German?

I don't start over from scratch. I pick up the adventure-world I'd already chosen and keep working on its terms. Workshop, marketplace, and campfire hold up. The harbor, on the other hand, feels too closed-off, the expedition too big. So the harbor becomes a portal, and the expedition becomes a research journey. I don't decide by any validated framework — in everyday school life, I make these calls intuitively, more often than not. What matters to me is whether the terms are self-explanatory to the students.

OUTPUT:
PRELIMINARY STATE Portal · Research Journey · Workshop · Marketplace · Campfire

For the first time, the former individual instruments have not just names, but roles within a shared system.

At this point I realize I've lost sight of my own original question. Actually, I wanted to find out how the five functions could be represented nonlinearly, or even spatially. Instead of continuing to think about axes, connections, and possible paths, I got stuck on the English terms. And now?

Maybe that's exactly why this detour leads me back to a sketch from August 25th. It shows an early state of the treehouse model. I've dropped the adventure world again, but its functions and some of its terms have stayed.

09

Back to a first sketch

The five functions give it a first order: I no longer see just individual tools, but the outline of a system.

***

The architecture stands. But it doesn't protect.
