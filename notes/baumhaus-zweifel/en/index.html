Field Note · English

Signal

Searching for protection, I destroy the school in my mind — and in the process stumble onto a force that ends up delivering not just answers, but an insight.

01

What I overlooked

"How could I not have thought of protection?" I ask myself, looking at the printed treehouse concept on my desk.
I wanted to make the treehouse immune to criticism, and in doing so I nearly destroyed the very thing I want to preserve: its openness. Protection, then, doesn't mean adding more rules, elements, or safeguards. Protection means setting boundaries and deliberately leaving something incomplete. That's the conclusion I reached yesterday. But does it hold everywhere? And is it even true?

02

Protection means limitation.

In my free time I often work on technical projects: electronic circuits that need safety mechanisms, an anemometer that raises the awning when the wind gets strong, or a control system for the motion sensors in the stairwell that only switches the light on below a set brightness level and turns it off again after a certain time.

In these projects I don't just plan what the control system should do. I also determine under what conditions it's allowed to react, when it shuts off, and what happens in case of an error or an exceeded limit.

The systems work differently. A fuse interrupts. The anemometer triggers a protective response. The stairwell light first checks whether it's even needed, then switches itself off again later on its own.

What they share isn't the same technical logic, but a design decision: I don't just determine what a system should do. I also determine when it's allowed to start, when it has to stop, and what it should refuse. So each of these control systems knows not only a "do this," but also a "not yet," a "no further," or a "stop."

Every one of these systems can say «No».

Then I think of the public school.
School has its own protective mechanisms too. There are requirements for safety, data protection, child protection, quality, and much more. At the same time, it's not short on goals, concepts, tools, and demands, either. They come from politics, from authorities, from research, from parents, from the school itself. Every single demand can be justified. Each one is meant to solve a problem or make school better. But who takes responsibility for the sum of them all?

School protects a great deal. But who protects school from having to take on more than it can handle?

I'm looking for the place where these demands get checked before they reach the classroom. Are there limits on time or volume? What happens if they're exceeded? Does an existing task disappear when a new one is added? Who checks, later on, whether something newly introduced actually made a difference? And who's allowed to say "stop"?

In school I find plenty of procedures for introducing something new. But I can barely find one for letting something go again.

I print out the treehouse and lay it on the table. I write the protective mechanisms on Post-its, to see where they should take effect. If I want to keep it from collapsing under the weight of its own possibilities, I have to teach it to say no.

I need a firewall. Not a rigid wall, but a permeable boundary that checks what's allowed in and what has to go back out. The treehouse can't take in everything and still has to stay open.

I write myself three initial rules: A new element gets an expiration date and stays only if I deliberately extend it. A capacity limit in the master prompt or YAML caps the number of tasks and tools; an effort alarm warns me about overloaded plans. And when something new is added, I have to decide what falls away in return. Protection also means leaving something out. I run through this last rule with an example: if I further develop a cantonal standard form, I can't simply add it on top of the old one. It either replaces it, or it stays out.

I add this protection to the concept. The treehouse is still lying in front of me on the desk. That's when the dissatisfaction comes back. The protection now shows me what must not happen to the treehouse. It doesn't answer my other question:

What's missing?

It's not another building block I'm looking for. This time the feeling runs deeper. I sense that something is missing – but I don't know what it is, or where to look for it. If I can't find the answer by building further, I have to change the direction of my search. I turn GPT around and stop using it as a co-constructive sparring partner. It shouldn't improve the treehouse anymore, it should attack it – its role: my sharpest critic and biggest fault-finder.

03

Advocatus diaboli

Two weeks later. An AI can always find a way to make an idea look good. While I work with it on the treehouse, I keep asking myself: Are its statements accurate? Its evaluations and judgments? Or is it just handing me the most convincing version of what I wanted to hear anyway?
I want a cognitive sparring partner, not a can of sugared whipped cream sweetening everything up. I sit in front of the computer for a long time, thinking about how to get GPT to argue against my idea instead of for it.

You:
**You:

**How can I be sure you're not just buttering me up? Control task: I'm giving you the assignment to check the model, the approach and the idea behind the treehouse for flaws, to criticize everything, and as far as I'm concerned, drag it through the mud. Do whatever it takes to make the traditional understanding of school look better by comparison. Not allowed: untruths, psychological tricks, ....

GPT starts without any warm-up. The first heading reads: 'Structural weaknesses: Why your model fails in practice.' Shortly after comes: 'Pedagogical flaws: Why your approach lets learners down.' With a directness I didn't expect, it exposes even the smallest weak points, dissects and fillets hours of my thinking in seconds, and serves me the worst parts on a silver platter. I find that – fascinating.

I read its points of criticism. Basically, there are three:

First it attacks the pedagogical openness: the treehouse relies too heavily on self-direction and neglects the systematic teaching of fundamentals. Those who benefit most are learners who already have support, language, and orientation. A model that supposedly wants to create more opportunities ends up reinforcing existing inequalities.
Then it attacks the practical feasibility: the treehouse is too expensive, too demanding, and barely scalable. It requires spaces, time, and teachers willing to go far beyond the usual. Parents expect reliability from school, not a permanent experiment.

Finally, GPT attacks the model systemically: the treehouse is idealistic and hard to measure. It promises learning adventures, personal responsibility, and future skills, but can't show whether any of that actually produces better learning. Maybe, it suggests, I'm riding a wave of AI, technology, and educational optimism, while good teaching really just needs a book and a good teacher.

By now GPT knows the authors I keep bringing up in our chats. So it doesn't surprise me that it reaches for Hattie of all people to point an empirical finger at the gaps in my treehouse. It continues its critique with: "Your learning adventure is missing the evidence."

It accuses me of ignoring findings on effectiveness. My treehouse, it says, ranks project-based learning at d = 0.30 and game-based learning at d = 0.34 above direct instruction at d = 0.59. GPT keeps going for a while and closes with two questions: "Where's the hard data? And the measurability?" It answers its own question right away: "Nowhere to be found!"

The criticism is harsh. But it's no proof that GPT is right, since I'm not checking its claims. It argues very convincingly – this time simply against me. I take a deep breath, because the criticism lands. Despite all the work that's gone into the treehouse, I find, interestingly, that I don't experience the criticism as an attack, and I don't see its harshness as a defeat either. Its response tells me the experiment worked.

I let the criticism sink in for a while. Then I notice an interesting process in myself: the water from the critical watering can doesn't put out my inner fire. Instead, new questions start sprouting from the ground.

Suddenly I discover questions in places where I've been thinking too fast up to now. The criticism doesn't answer these questions, and one of them in particular stays with me stubbornly: How do I prevent exactly those learners who need orientation most urgently from being left alone?

04

Destroy the school and you'll find what you're looking for

The last round of criticism gave me good questions, but I still can't get further in my search for the unknown. The individual building blocks are consistent in themselves, but something isn't right about how they interact.

This Monday I keep studying these connections between the elements and ask myself whether there isn't also something around them – something that enables growth and development? But searching for this good thing gets me nowhere. So I ask myself: what happens if I reverse the direction of my search again?

I'm not looking for what makes the treehouse grow, but asking what could destroy it: its elements, its connections, and the whole ground they stand on. What brings growth and development to a halt?
If I identify these damaging factors, I can invert them. That's my logic, anyway.
And if I know what destroys school, maybe I also know what makes it grow.

So I write a prompt and I'm fine with it – you can tell from the prompt that I read a lot of Tom Clancy novels back in the day.

You:
**You:

**If a CIA intelligence agent had the task of destroying the school from within. E.g., disinformation, subversion, … How would he go about it? What are the technical terms for this kind of psychological warfare?

Reading the answer, I realize I've opened a door that would have been better left closed. GPT introduces historically documented terms from subversion, psychological operations, and hybrid warfare, and transfers them onto the school.

In this thought experiment, the agent doesn't attack the school from the outside. His goal is to weaken it from within by undermining trust in the education system. GPT describes in detail various methods such as disinformation, cognitive warfare, active measures, gray-zone tactics, and cultural subversion. Interested, I read through these Cold War strategies and marvel at the strategic significance GPT attributes to public schools. School appears here not just as a place of learning, but as a carrier of shared values, norms, and cultural identity.

What has read like a fictional novel so far suddenly shifts my perception when GPT starts listing possible tactics:
Overload curricula with ever-new, hard-to-verify competencies. Create competency inflation: many terms, little clarity, and ever-greater room for interpretation.
Burden teachers with documentation requirements and over-academicize assessment. In the end, what counts is no longer what works, but what can be proven.
Ideologically polarize school and instruction: "left-green" versus "conservative." Instrumentalize topics like gender, climate, or migration for culture-war battles, stoke mistrust, and split teachers.
Push individualization until the class fragments: separate learning paths, digital self-directed learning, fewer shared experiences, and ever less social bonding.
Systematically sow doubt about teachers' professional competence. Turn isolated cases into proof of the school system's failure: "The curriculum is failing." "Kids can't read anymore." Pit parents and teachers against each other.

"But that's not the Cold War anymore," I think. "Isn't this like today? Isn't this the here and now?" Then I stop myself. Haven't I just ordered a manual for destruction from GPT? Not through violence, not loudly, but slowly, patiently, and unnoticed.

"Why the school?" I ask GPT, and it explains that the school is a key structure of a stable society. If it's weakened, its argument goes, social cohesion, democracy, and economic development suffer in the long run – in short, the future viability of an entire nation. That makes sense to me.

I keep thinking and ask myself what GPT could mean by "school." The students, the parents, the school administrations, authorities, the bureaucracy – or the teachers?

Maybe teachers are the most sensitive point in the system. I keep playing out the thought: if I wanted to weaken the school, could I start with the teachers? If they were systematically weakened, would that affect the quality of instruction? And what if these consequences remained invisible at first? What if they only showed up years later – in the career opportunities and development prospects of individual young people and, taken together, perhaps even in society as a whole? Maybe too late to still recognize where the chain began?

Maybe a country's future
begins with
how it thinks about its teachers.

05

Between lockstep and isolation

The thought is now out there. I start with a development that has been on my mind for a while: what does increasing standardization do to teachers?

At the same time, teaching is supposed to become ever more individualized at the level of the students. That seems contradictory: more differentiation for learners, more standardization for schools and teachers.

I admit it: I don't write the next prompt in a relaxed state of mind. It's manipulative and leading, and I practically build the answer I want right into the question.

You:
**You:

**Schools are becoming increasingly conformist and standardized, which leads especially engaged teachers to quietly check out and just go through the motions. Does that support the erosion of schools?

Of course GPT confirms that excessive standardization can erode a school from within. If engaged teachers mentally check out, the school loses its living professional substance. Teachers stop experiencing themselves as professionals and start experiencing themselves as executors of a system. Their own ideas, experience, and intuition get replaced by directives, checklists, and rubrics. Being able to prove something becomes more important than whether it actually works. Behind every new control instrument there's also a message: we don't trust your professional judgment. And when every deviation first has to be justified, documented, and safeguarded, a new idea quickly turns into an added risk.

Maybe the controls are mainly meant to secure a minimum standard. They're supposed to prevent individual teachers from doing too little or shirking the shared mandate. But they may well hit the wrong people first. Not the ones who just go through the motions anyway, but the engaged ones: the ones who try things out, take on responsibility, and do more than required. It's precisely them who lose their room to maneuver as the rules tighten.

Maybe they withdraw. Maybe at some point they too start just going through the motions. Maybe they leave. And with them, part of the school's pedagogical fire disappears.

You:

Individualization as a tool of erosion. In a heterogeneous class, diversity is a fact and differentiation is a means to an end. Would everything then have to run by one single scheme? Uniformity and conformity.

GPT reins in my exaggeration. Individualization, it says, is not in itself a tool of erosion. In a heterogeneous class, differentiation is necessary. Children bring different starting points, interests, and levels of learning with them. Not responding to that wouldn't be fairness — it would be equal treatment at any cost.

It becomes a problem, it says, when differentiation tips over into radical individualization: the teacher ends up managing 24 separate learning paths, shared experiences and goals disappear, and a class turns into a group of learners who just happen to sit in the same room.

The promised freedom doesn't automatically distribute itself fairly, either. Children who get support at home and can already organize their own learning path tend to find their footing. Others may lose exactly the orientation that school is supposed to give them.

Education then risks turning from a jointly shared public good into a private project of individual learners.

That's where my thought experiment tips over. Too much uniformity can push teachers into just going through the motions. But boundless individualization can overwhelm them from the other side — and weaken what's shared at the same time. I'm standing between two dangers that don't cancel each other out, but can both take effect at once.

Neither pole gives me the orientation I'm looking for. I want movable post-its, but not arbitrariness. A shared structure, but not lockstep uniformity. Differentiation, but not 24 separate learning paths.

So the treehouse needs more than just post-its I can add, move, or remove. It needs a fixed reference point against which I can check decisions. A direction. The question is no longer just: what's allowed to change?

What has to stay,
when everything else is allowed to change?

06

The direction stays

The idea of the North Star isn't new. I first wrote it down on the evening of August 25th, while explaining the individual elements of the Treehouse to Mistral. Next to the treetop, I wrote: What is the ultimate goal? What will still hold true in twenty years, once technologies, methods, and conditions have long since changed? What is our North Star?

Back then, it stayed a question inside a single prompt. The next day I work on a long chat, and Mistral asks me a closing question:

**Mistral:

**If you could preserve one thing from your model for eternity – what would it be? (Not because it's perfect, but because it moves you the most.)

My answer is two words:

You:
**You:

**The North Star

Mistral writes:

A North Star needs no advertising.
It's simply there – for those who look for it.

The term isn't new. But its meaning shifts for me. The evening before, the North Star was still a question inside the treetop. Now it becomes the answer to what must stay when everything else is allowed to change. The North Star isn't another element I can stick onto the Treehouse. It isn't a method or a tool, because those come and go. Teaching has to be able to change, because people and conditions change.

What exactly this North Star contains, I haven't finally determined. But in my sketches so far, five orientation points keep coming up: building competence, self-efficacy, learning how to learn, transfer, and collaboration. Its purpose becomes clear: it gives me a fixed reference point against which I can test changes, without prescribing a specific path. The Treehouse should serve people – not the other way around. The path may change. The direction stays.

07

The elements begin to interact

The North Star gives me a direction and reminds me what should stay when methods, tools, or individual building blocks change.

But it doesn't yet answer another question: What happens between the building blocks when I move one of them?

You:

I want to link the diagnostic tests to learning goals with building block 2. The learning-goal cards are linked to building block 1, the "starter kit." Somehow none of it fits together. [...] If I have to create six building blocks for every topic, that overwhelms not just me but the students too. That many tools is great in theory, but it can also be confusing. I don't want linear teaching.

In early August I built the Treehouse around the course material. Out of that came individual building blocks: learning-goal cards, diagnostic tests, and tasks. All of them are connected to the course material. But not only to that.

I stuck these onto the desk as Post-its and asked myself: "What's going on between them?" And while rearranging the Post-its, I noticed that I never move a single one without thinking of at least one other. They act on each other.

I notice that the learning-goal cards and the diagnostic test are connected. The learning goals determine what the test checks. The test shows which tasks are needed. And while working on these tasks, I notice I need to rephrase a learning goal. None of that is new to me. But what happens if I use AI to make the boundaries between the building blocks more permeable, so the connections become more visible?

Months later, as I keep working with the same building blocks, I first call this interplay resonance, without knowing how to describe this "something" between the elements. Is it a connection? A force? I don't know.

With these building blocks, I'm not simply developing modules I can slap onto my teaching however I like. They aren't add-ons. The building blocks can change, develop, and grow. That's the idea behind the treehouse:
It isn't a rigid building — it sways, it responds, it lives.

You:

"First of all, none of the elements should be viewed in isolation. […] Everything has its meaning and purpose, is based on something, and is intended for something further."

These changes to building blocks, and their effect on other elements, can cause something new to emerge from their interplay — something that wasn't there before. Is that generative? Together with resonance, I try to combine the two as resonant-generative. That describes best what I'm looking for. So I shorten it to: RESOGENA. It's not a theory and not a finished model. It's simply my working name, for now, for a phenomenon I know from the classroom but had never really thought about. I think I need to dig even deeper. Somewhere in the learning process between person, task, and world — it has to be there. And I picture a field representing this something.

08

Should I stop?

Over the past few weeks, I've been asking myself more and more often why I'm doing this.
It's not part of my job description. I don't make any money from it. Nobody expects anything from me. Not a single person knows about this project. The kids experience different lessons and say they're cool. But they don't know what's behind it.

Am I stuck in a loop?

The further I develop the treehouse, the more it also changes me — and the further my ideas about school and teaching drift from the framework and reality I actually work in. I don't know whether public school has changed over the last few years, or whether I have changed over the last few months. But my path and the path of public school no longer seem to lead in the same direction. The gap between what is and what should be keeps growing.

The effort has long since reached dimensions I never expected. At the same time, the pull to discover something new tempts me, and so I waver between stopping and continuing. That's a decision I have to make myself. AI can't take this decision off my hands. But it can examine my reasons, surface counterarguments, and show me if I'm overlooking something. So I run one last counter-check and write:

You:
**You:

**Honest and critical feedback: Should I keep pursuing this project idea? Assess its meaningfulness, necessity, complexity, future viability, and uniqueness — and also consider criteria I might be overlooking.

GPT weighs the project. It speaks in favor of continuing: the treehouse connects elements that are otherwise often considered separately, and it directs attention to questions that matter for the future of school. At the same time, it warns of the growing complexity, the enormous time investment, and the risk of developing a model that, in the end, only works for me. The answer is more nuanced than earlier assessments, and yet it doesn't recommend anything. Instead, GPT suggests a shift in perspective: I should imagine looking back on this moment from the year 2040.

**ChatGPT:

**Imagine it's 2040 and […]
"… you didn't do it."
"… you did it, but failed."
"… you did it, and it works."

I read GPT's answer and realize the three retrospectives don't answer my question. But while reading, I notice that my decision has long since been made.

I keep going.

Simply because I feel like I'm living in a moment where something new is emerging. And whether the treehouse is ahead of its time, as GPT says, I don't know. But I get to witness it happen. That's enough for me.

09

personal workshop

I've decided to keep going. But when I look at the treehouse again, a familiar unease returns. I see the elements and the connections between them. I see how they react to each other and can change one another. But even this networked system doesn't set itself in motion on its own. This time, no additional tool and no extra method is missing. What's missing is something that makes all these possibilities come alive in the first place.

You:

New question: Do you see any element in the treehouse model, however small or insignificant, even if it's only remotely connected to the overarching goal in the "Tree Crown" field, that is missing from the model?

GPT suggests a seed or a sprout – a small element that stands for the tree's future. At first the answer seems almost too simple. Then I realize what I like about this image: the seed isn't another module I have to build into the treehouse. It stands for the person who sets their own possibilities in motion in the first place. For curiosity. For inner motivation. For the willingness to pursue a question even though no one assigned it.
The treehouse can open spaces, provide tools, and enable connections. But it can't force anyone to let something grow out of it. Maybe this is where the difference lies between a system that merely functions and a person who develops something out of their own drive.

The image of the seed turns my gaze back to myself. What actually set this treehouse in motion? I never wanted to develop a model. I wanted to prepare lessons. But every answer gave rise to a new question. Every element led to another connection. I kept building because I wanted to understand what happens in my teaching and what I can change. And that's exactly why it isn't a model that can be compared to others. This is my workshop. Not a model carved in marble, not a monument that gets built and then defended. It stays movable. I can check it, shift it, or remove it. What works today is allowed to disappear tomorrow.

Its individual elements aren't new. Learning goals, feedback, portfolios, and learning workshops have existed for a long time. Their combination isn't automatically unique either. What matters is why I choose them, how I connect them with each other, and when I'm willing to change them again.
Every instance of teaching takes shape under different conditions. What fits my class doesn't have to fit another. That's why the visible result can't simply be transferred from one place to another.

Individual elements can be adopted. But anyone who copies only the visible result without going through the thought process behind it builds a hollow replica.

Only in hindsight do I realize that this is what became the treehouse. Not as a plan. Not as a finished theory. But as a trace of my own thinking. I want answers. I want to solve puzzles. I want to understand enough to make good decisions in my teaching. The treehouse gathers the questions that accompany me along the way: What do the children in front of me need? What may change? And what has to stay? It's my very own way of understanding all of this a little better.

The treehouse?
It's just a visible form of my thinking, questioning, and building.
