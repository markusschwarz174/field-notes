Ubuntugogy

What starts with 20 concepts leads to an unfinished spreadsheet — where completeness, in the end, matters less than the insight it produces.

01

Twenty against one

It's November 4th. Fairly far down in ChatGPT's sidebar, I spot a chat titled "Didactic models worldwide." It takes me back to early September. Back then, my lesson prep first turned into didactic building blocks, then into a teaching idea, and finally into a full teaching concept.

At the time, I asked myself how scientifically grounded this treehouse idea actually was. My thinking back then was shaped by a competitive "better"/"worse" pattern. Out of that, a certain ambition grew in me.

At the start, I only analyzed 20 didactic models from Europe and checked which elements of them were already present in the treehouse and which were missing. I filled in the gaps in the treehouse and had it list the next models. Very quickly, things went international.

Here's what one of these comparisons between the treehouse and a didactic model looked like:

I didn't know this didactic concept. I also didn't take the time to look into it more closely, because I was only interested in the "missing elements."

It was a race, a chase after an ever-higher coverage rate: 65%, 82%, 91%. The AI told me: "Your model covers 85% of didactic models worldwide. If you add this or that element, coverage will rise to 88%."
It was a spiral, until at some point I realized: I will never reach 100%. What I have in front of me today, on November 4th, is the record of that chase — and its abandonment. The effort was considerable, and so was the disillusionment. But the insight was bigger:

I pitted my treehouse against the world, and in the end I had to give up. What's left? "A silly idea," I think today.

02

Another chase for 100%?

I scroll through the very long chat and see all the didactic models and teaching concepts. I no longer want to build the best teaching concept. I also don't care where the best school is or what the best teacher's name is.

What interests me is the data behind the didactic models: their names, their origins, and above all their individual elements. Just as I've already built databases on task formats and didactic levels, I want to build a database of didactic models. Later on, it should help me develop teaching materials and lesson plans in a targeted way. Without a reliable data foundation, the AI is fishing in the open sea when it generates content. With the right datasets, I give it part of the framework: which models, terms, and elements should even be allowed to flow into the answer.

I open the chat and Excel. I quickly logged the first learning concepts in a table, and after that it grew fast with more models from Europe and the Anglo-Saxon world. I also capture school-development topics. Then I save the file and wonder whether I can just hand Claude the whole job: "Create a complete library of all didactic teaching models and school concepts worldwide — as a downloadable Excel spreadsheet."
I try it, but for Claude the world ends after twelve models. After its second apology — and again the list ends at twelve models — I realize there must be a limit I have no influence over.
Before I keep expanding my table, I want to check it first: Is the table even structured consistently? Can the existing columns hold all the information I need?

03

The first attempt fails

Unfortunately, I asked myself that question too late.

The result is a disaster. In front of me sits an incomplete Excel table. Do I start over or fix the table? I decide to fix it — and I don't start with the individual entries, but with the framework: the columns.

You:
First, I'd like you to give me feedback on the column headers. Which ones would you delete, rename, or add?

We work our way column by column through the framework of the future table.

Claude checks all 13 columns for how they're actually used. That's how it happens that Claude wants to keep fields like "concept_id," "branch," and "element," and delete unused columns like "year" and "name_ger." Others get renamed or merged.

I put in my veto. The year and the German label stay in, even if the English label sits right next to them. There's another decision that can't be solved by deleting or renaming either: What do I do with names whose German translation is understandable but drifts far from the original?

ChatGPT: ORIGINAL NAME ENGLISH VERSION Universal Design for Learning Universal Design for Learning Flipped Classroom Flipped Classroom Response to Intervention Multi-Tiered System of Support

While checking, I discover another problem: didactic models rarely have one clear birthdate — usually they have several at once.

OUTPUT:
Comment: PBL emerged in the late 1960s at McMaster University; the term was coined by Barrows in 1974, and the canonical standard work appeared in 1980 (Barrows & Tamblyn). → 1969 is plausible as the origin year, the literature year is more likely 1974/1980. (McMaster/Barrows 1980; Schmidt & O'Grady history study; ERIC overview)

GPT offers me three options: 1974 for the coining of the term, 1980 for the standard work — or both years together. I can't keep all three years in the table. The column calls for the year of publication, so I go with 1974: that's the year Victor Neufeld and Howard Barrows publish their article on the "McMaster Philosophy." The curricular practice at McMaster University already begins in 1969.

I look at the comment on problem-based learning again and can't help smiling: "emerged in the late 1960s." Listening to some voices in education today, you'd think it was a brand-new invention. This isn't the only time reading a didactic card gives me that thought. And it won't be the last time a year makes a supposedly new idea look pretty old.

04

Round by round, beyond Europe

Three days later, the column structure is settled and the records are "repaired" enough that I can keep building. For that, Claude is supposed to design a rule and give me the results in a form I can copy straight into my table.

You:
Yes, create me a copy-paste table for columns A through G. Rule: Columns A and B stay the same. Column C you keep numbering, so every concept gets its own number. Column F you only fill in if it's an American/English concept. Column G (German) you can fill in either way — either as the original name or as a translation of the English original title.

Claude suggests something called a TSV file. I have no idea what a TSV file is, and I say yes anyway. It creates such a table, and I notice that row 1 is missing its number. After that I find missing years, and one model number shows up more than once because "Konstruktivismus" with a trailing space doesn't show up in the mapping.

After a few rounds of corrections – and just short of giving up – it works. Claude delivers a clean block that I can paste straight into Excel.

From then on it's automatic: round by round, I add the data into Excel; first DACH, then GB/USA, then international. Here's what one of these TSV blocks looks like – ready for the direct route into Excel:

05

623 rows later: a starting prompt for the next chat

These are the 70 didactic models, concepts, and school-development topics from September. Together they make up 623 individual elements in the Excel list. That sounds like a lot – and it is. Still, I want to keep going, and Claude already has a plan:

Claude: 📋 RECOMMENDED PROCESS: Round 1: Asia (25 concepts) Round 2: Oceania (15 concepts) Round 3: Scandinavia (15 concepts) Round 4: Latin America (10 concepts) Round 5: Instructional quality (10 concepts) Round 6: Learning theories (10 concepts) Round 7: Historical (10 concepts) → ~95 new concepts

But the chat has grown too long. I need a new one, and I have Claude write a starting prompt for it.

You:
Du: Read this chat. I want to expand my Excel table with more didactic, educational-psychology, and pedagogical models, but also school-development topics or something on instructional quality. Worldwide. I need a starting prompt that I can use in a new chat, one where the data comes out in the column order so I only have to copy-paste. Your role? Come up with a proposal.

This is probably the first time I have an AI write me such a specific starting prompt. Later I'll come to call these handoffs between chats "bridge prompts."
Claude produces not just the starting prompt, but also instructions with a role, a process, and a quality check. It also gives a brief summary:

Its tally impresses me: 304 missing sources researched. Five examples are listed, then just: "… and 299 more." Who could do that? What other technology could examine a table with more than 500 rows for me, spot the gaps, and above all, carry on my task on its own? I don't know of any.

But something else becomes clear to me too: before I keep collecting, I need to check what's already in the table. I ask myself: who actually checks whether all of this is correct?

06

Now checking matters, not searching

I switch from Claude to GPT, upload the Excel table, and ask it to check the first 520 rows for accuracy.

You:
Du: Check rows 2 through 520 and columns A through G for consistency and accuracy.

At first, GPT understands "accuracy" differently than I do: missing values, duplicates, stray spaces, and inconsistent spelling. But a different question is on my mind: is the content Claude wrote into the table actually correct? I word the task more precisely.

You:
Du: Find all rows for concepts 1 and 2 (column B). Check columns C through G for accuracy (internet research). If you're not sure, mark it with an asterisk. In column H, check the elements. Are they complete and correct?

GPT is supposed to check every concept online, flag uncertainties, and present the results so I can drop them straight into Excel. It works through the table concept by concept — and since Montessori holds number 1, the check starts with Maria Montessori.

This way I can see at a glance whether everything checks out and what still needs adding. But this result is hardly something I can transfer into my Excel list. I ask GPT for a copy-pasteable version:

After that I keep going concept by concept, checking for missing and unassigned elements at the same time. But suddenly GPT starts commenting on the concepts:

OUTPUT:
Concept 15 – Berlin & Hamburg Model (Heimann/Schulz/Otto) The Berlin Model (1960s) structures lesson planning through condition and decision fields; the Hamburg Model refines and extends this perspective. It is considered an analytical planning framework (not a method program) and shaped German-language didactics for decades.

For GPT these are context one-liners: an ultra-short, historically situating side note. I enjoy reading them, and sometimes they raise questions of their own for me:

You:
Du: […] by the way: how did you come up with "Rosenshine condensed evidence-based practices on explicit instruction in his Principles of Instruction (2010/2012)"? Wasn't Rosenshine already dead by then?

ChatGPT: Thought for 15s […] On Rosenshine: No, he had not died by 2012. Barak Rosenshine lived until May 22, 2017. His well-known overview "Principles of Instruction: Research-Based Strategies That All Teachers Should Know" appeared in Spring 2012 in American Educator. There is also a 2010 IAE pamphlet with a similar compilation (10 principles).

Yes, sometimes you can be wrong.

07

Out of the back-and-forth grows a master prompt

Out of the correction loops, something new slowly grows: not just a better answer, but a reusable template. I no longer have to explain to GPT, for every single concept, which columns to check, how to flag uncertainty, and in what form to return the result. Those rules now live in the master prompt. All I have to decide is what GPT should do: check existing entries online, or search for models and elements still missing from the table. The master prompt gets its own versions, a guide, a README, and even a changelog. The two modes are called: Add and Verify.

08

The leap

A few rounds later I come across another concept I've already heard of:

What surprises me isn't the concept, but the note 'Japan.' What surprises me even more is my own reaction: did I really feel that didactic models only existed in Europe and the US? Honestly, I have to admit: I never gave it any thought — until now.

"Japan?" I ask myself. "What didactic models exist there?" This question opens a door not just to another country, but to the whole world. I've been a teacher for almost 30 years, and I never once asked myself how a teacher in Japan, Brazil, or Namibia actually teaches.

In my research I had covered Germany and Austria, but completely forgot Italy and France. Instead of going back to fill those gaps, I jump straight to Asia: "Give me 25 concepts from Asia."

I read up on the Japanese concept of Lesson Study and learn how teachers make a single lesson the shared object of their work. Together they design one lesson, observe it live, and then take it apart together afterward. What emerges is collective improvement instead of lone-wolf effort. And me? I'm sitting alone in front of my screen, collecting didactic models.
With almost every model or concept, the dates catch up with me again: late 19th century, the 2000s, 2007. None of it is new. It was just unknown to me until now.

09

The list becomes a world map

And I send GPT off to search for didactic models from Africa.

TaRL doesn't start with age or grade level, but with the child's actual learning level. Ubuntugogy shifts the focus from individual achievement to connectedness, dignity, and mutual responsibility. One is a scalable support method, the other more of a guiding pedagogical principle. Both perspectives take me far from where my search began in early September: back then, I pitted my treehouse against the world.

And Ubuntugogy? It doesn't even ask who wins.

Scandinavia: Finland and Sweden – flat-out forgotten on my first pass. I read about Phenomenon-Based Learning, where students temporarily work across subjects on a shared phenomenon. About Friluftsliv, which moves learning outdoors. And about Trialogic Learning, where knowledge takes shape in something created together: a model, a design, a document.

I'm not writing these lines because I already knew this, but because I'm reading about it for the first time. How was I supposed to look for a book whose existence I didn't even know about?

Central and South America: In Brazil I come across Paulo Freire. The same idea, for over half a century now – asking questions instead of depositing knowledge. Education shouldn't just fill people with material; it should help them understand their own reality and talk about it together.
Next door in Colombia, participation gets very practical: Escuela Nueva combines multi-grade village schools with self-guided learning materials, student government, and real tasks from the community. Two different approaches – but both treat learners not as recipients, but as participants.

Australia and New Zealand: At first I think I forgot this region. Then I check – "Round 2: Oceania" has been in the table all along.
In Queensland I come across Productive Pedagogies, and with it the question of what actually makes good teaching. In New South Wales, teachers observe each other in the classroom and develop their practice together in Quality Teaching Rounds. New Zealand's Teaching as Inquiry turns this into a cycle: focus, investigate, act, evaluate. With every region, my table grows. And with every region, my idea that I could ever make it complete grows smaller.

10

What's left of the list

On December 11, 2025, I've reached row 1465. The database still needs cleaning up. But for now, I'm stopping.

I'm stopping — not because I'm done, but because other things are becoming more important.

I scroll through the list. Name by name, year by year, country by country. For minutes. Somewhere between row 800 and row 1200, it becomes clear to me how small my didactic microcosm had been until now. I knew how little I know. Now I see it in front of me. What began as a hunt for completeness gives way to something else: humility and respect.

When people present their teaching model as the one true or best approach, I listen differently now. This model, too, I think, is one possible answer to the question of how children can learn. One of hundreds — and I only know the ones I've gathered so far.

And then I recognize it — a déjà vu: back in early September, it was the treehouse racing against an unreachable limit. And now I'm trying it again — bigger, and against the whole world. Coverage keeps growing, and 100 percent will always stay out of reach.

On my PC now sits this file with all the teaching models, named "C11xx_14_MODELLE_WELT_V5.0_251211.xlsx". A file I can delete or archive. This incredible diversity of models, concepts, and guiding ideas in my head, though — that I can't.

Three examples stick with me: Jugyō Kenkyū ('Lesson Study') from Japan, Ubuntugogy from Africa, and Paulo Freire's 'Pedagogy of the Oppressed' from Brazil.
And I ask myself: Is the model from Japan related to the one from Brazil? Is one of these concepts the answer to a question in my own classroom? Or does an answer maybe only emerge from a combination of them?

Something has shifted in me.

To me, these are three completely different starting points, and yet they all circle around a similar question: How can children and young people learn and grow? Maybe we're closer on this than my European perspective led me to assume. And one thing becomes clear to me:

How many people on this earth are searching for a good school for our children.

And that is something incredibly beautiful.
