DIDACTIC TEMPLATES

38 didactic levels become a working list. Then I look for adjustment points for my teaching — and land on didactic templates.

01

Scoring 38 Didactic Levels – the Plan

I'm picking up right where my first milestone left off: the "big list" is done. Over several rounds I collected and consolidated a "Global List" that ended up with 38 didactic levels across 6 clusters. On top of that come tags and four cross-cutting dimensions: digitality, ethics & responsibility, agility & openness to experimentation, and critical reflection. The full list doesn't belong here. It's the forest. In this note, what interests me is the next step: which few levels make it onto the short list, and why?

Because the big list is too much for everyday classroom life, and it was always meant to be just a first step. I want to use the didactic levels to differentiate tasks or learning environments in my teaching. For that I don't need a forest of 38 didactic levels — I need a small selection of roughly six to ten.

And now I want to make that reduction happen: I'll pick from the big list to create a "short list" — the "LOCAL LIST." This is meant to become my working tool for everyday teaching: didactic levels not as rigid on/off switches, but as dials. I give GPT a brief description of my goal:

You:
You: Top 6–9 levels become «Local List 1.1». Adjustment points. 1–2 examples per adjustment point (to anchor the expectation). For each selected level a clear «dial scale». That means 2–3 […]

But can you even evaluate a didactic level? I look for criteria and find no scientific basis for things like the promptability of didactic levels, so I fall back on core practical questions and GPT's recommendations: How strong is the lever for learning? How well can the level be observed or used across subjects? And above all: how reliably can it be translated into AI prompts? This is groundwork for a scoring process I want to hand off to GPT to run. My plan: GPT builds it, runs it, and suggests — I check and validate. Each didactic level gets rated against these weighted criteria.

The table is more or less entirely built by GPT and works with six criteria, a 0–2 scale, and weightings. It should assess each didactic level against these criteria, calculate the total, and sort by score. One thing matters to me here: only the didactic levels get scored. The cross-cutting dimensions are not selected and not rated. They stay alongside as a reference grid. The 6–9 levels with a high score, low redundancy, and broad applicability should form my short list — my «Local List». After that, I want 2–3 adjustment points for each selected level, each with 1–2 anchor examples. Those I then want to use in teaching and in GPT prompts. That's the plan.

02

What the Scoring Produces

I have GPT rate the 38 didactic levels against the six criteria, add up the weighted values, and present everything as an overview.

The tags stay visible in the table. [DIG] marks levels with a connection to digitality, [A/D] marks levels that can be used both analog and digital, and [FUT] marks connections to future skills. These tags don't add extra points to the scoring. They just help me see at a glance which levels carry particular connections.

Again, the result is an unwieldy mammoth table that I have to zoom out in Excel just to see it whole. I don't treat the table as mathematical truth or as an objective ranking of didactic quality — and it's not about finding the "best" didactic level either. It's simply about using a working tool to find the levels that might suit me best. I scan the scoring table and check the levels briefly: no dealbreakers. I move on and sort the table by total score.

03

Eleven Levels for My Classroom

I look at the full list and focus on the levels at the top. I then add the didactic levels with the maximum score of 18, plus the two with 17 and 16 points, to my «short list». The three didactic levels with 15 points I keep in reserve, as a backup. That gives me an «8 + 3 list».

I copy the 11 didactic levels into a new table and look at them. So this is what it looks like, I think. I notice that the levels don't cover the clusters evenly: Cluster 2 is in reserve and Cluster 6 is missing entirely. Then I see «MEDIA CRITICISM» sitting right next to «MEDIA». I briefly consider whether I should adjust something, but I move on — I can always do that later.

I now have my very own working list — for my classroom, my teaching, and one goal: to better support my students.

Note: These four cross-cutting dimensions are not scored. They are kept as a review framework alongside the selected Local List.

Alongside the Local List, I carry the four cross-cutting dimensions forward: Digitality, Ethics & Responsibility, Agility & Openness to Experimentation, and Critical Reflection. They don't compete with the Local List. The Local List shows which instructional levels I can actually work on. The cross-cutting dimensions show which questions I can't afford to forget while working on those levels.

In parallel with this chat, I'm working on a second Local List in MISTRAL. It's similar and also covers eight levels, but weights the clusters differently. I'm leaving it aside — I want to keep going with the GPT version.

04

Adjustable Parameters for My Teaching

I look at the instructional levels "Content," "Representation," and "Differentiation" and think about what adjustable parameters each level might have. What happens when I turn those dials? Left — right. How can I use them with an AI? And so I signal to GPT that we're moving on.

You:
Du: Yes, next step: table with the adjustable parameters and the examples.

These are the first rows of my parameters table:

From here, the word "parameter" gets concrete. For me, an instructional level is the field where I can turn a dial. An adjustable parameter is the specific change within that field: more or less support, a different format, a different product, a different social arrangement. So I'm not looking for the perfect task — I'm looking for the places where I can deliberately change a task.

So what do I have now? I have a tool that lets me deliberately adjust a lesson plan, a learning environment, or a single task. I can control a level directly through its adjustable parameters. I just need to learn how to prompt that with GPT — and then I want to observe and measure the effect in the classroom.

05

The First Practical Test: Parameters for a Task Set

I've built a parameters table for 11 instructional levels and want to test it on the current unit "Our Earth." Can I actually change a single task or a task set? I ask GPT to take the perspective of an upper-school teacher and assess which levels and parameters would work for a task prompt on the social studies topic "Our Earth." And its response is, in a way, interesting.

AI:
ChatGPT: I'm responding as if I were actually a teacher of a 2nd-year secondary class (13-year-olds) in 2024 — with GPT as an assistant for creating a task set for the subject RZG (Space, Time, Society), topic "Our Earth."

Not because it did something impressive, but because only after the response did I realize I had just asked an AI to treat another AI as its "assistant." A brief thought in my head — nothing earth-shattering — and on we go.

Its response to the perspective shift is short, tight, and clear:

OUTPUT:
ChatGPT: Ensure learning effectiveness for 13-year-olds (high leverage), must be didactically controllable and observable, must remain translatable into GPT prompts.

I read the three points and think: "One for the student level, one for the teacher level, one for the AI-technology level. Smart." Less smart, I find its next output on "Selected Levels (Top Starter Pack for GPT Prompts)" with the 🔝 symbol:

It presents ten didactic levels from the original GLOBAL List. Some of them aren't even on the short list. On top of that, a task prompt with a traditional linear structure:

CODE:
ChatGPT: Prompt scaffold: You are a teaching assistant for a 2nd year lower secondary class (13-year-olds). Topic: Our Earth – structure, continents, climate, human spaces. Create a task set (6–8 tasks) with the following structure: Introduction – activate motivation & prior knowledge (C1-content + C3-action + sequencing). Development – reading maps, interpreting data, explaining connections (cognitive). …

At the end, a rationale for its level selection follows, along with a summary conclusion:

This output is a warning sign. Generic phrasings like «I would» or buzzwords like «didactic backbone» are signals to me that the chat is running out of steam. GPT has checked out. I could keep going, or I could trust my gut that this chat is dead. Bad answers will keep producing bad answers. I don't yet know why this happens or what the cause and possible fix might be. Am I writing something wrong?

I close the chat and start a new conversation. It irritates me a little, because it always means picking up the thread all over again in the new chat.

06

New Attempt

After three turns I'm back to where I left off. And I can push further:

You:
Du: What about collaborative forms? Participation? Community Learning?

Of course, GPT produced its output faster than I could finish typing the prompt — and it doesn't surprise me that it had already built a new table with the six relevant levels for collaboration and participation, pulled from the full list of 38 didactic levels. Once again I'm well on my way to overload.

I already have eight plus three levels, and now GPT is suggesting six new "relevant levels." It's starting to remind me of an education authority that always piles something "relevant" on top at the start of the school year. Unlike with education authorities, I do actually like some of the AI's suggestions. And this is where GPT shows a clear strength: it sorts and combines knowledge fast and presents it convincingly.

The overview is simply clean and coherent. I study it and try to work out in my head what theoretical model might be behind it. ICAP by Chi & Wylie? Maybe. Either way, I could easily see myself building this overview into a task generator.

07

One Word Sets Something in Motion

And then comes the perfectly ordinary end of a chat round: the summary conclusion — and a single word is enough to trigger something in me. These are the rare moments. The really good ones.

It's the last word in GPT's response: «Set.» Three letters. I know the word perfectly well. But I notice my brain starting to fire. The mail carrier in my head can't deliver the package — not because he can't find the right drawer and I have to re-read the last sentence several times, but because there's no drawer yet for this new meaning of the word. EUREKA might be too big a word, but the moment is real. Not because the AI delivers a truth or a ready-made solution, but because it shows me a door to a new way of thinking.

I don't think GPT is aware of it.
Maybe a similar idea already exists somewhere in the world, and whether it's in OpenAI's training data I have no way of knowing. The word «Set» is the statistically plausible continuation of the preceding text — for GPT, it's simply the most natural next step. I'm grateful for it.

I re-read the last line — «→ that's your Collaborative Set» — and every time it feels like a radio transmission from Major Tom:

This also changes how I understand Core-Sets. A Core-Set isn't just a selection of levels. It also has to remain readable through the cross-cutting dimensions. When I build a Core-Set for collaboration, product work, or exploratory learning, I don't only ask: Which levels do I combine? I also ask: Where does digitality come in? Where do ethical questions arise? How much agility do I allow? And where does critical reflection need to happen?

08

Core-Sets as Didactic Blends

With the term «Collaborative Core Set», GPT introduces a label — and I recognize a usable template in it. A set isn't just a selection; it's a reusable template. I think: «If the clusters aren't just sorting containers but thematic spice jars, I should be able to mix my own blends from them for different situations.»
Combinations of levels from different clusters, optimized as Core-Sets for specific learning scenarios. Not as rigid didactic settings from a recipe book, but as a design aid.

And with the help of AI I could … — I stop writing. A new section of the conversation with GPT begins, where we discuss AI-assisted didactics and how it and Learning Analytics might one day dynamically adapt such Core-Sets. That's exciting — and firmly in the realm of future music, here in October 2025. First I want to understand whether simple, pre-built Core-Sets work in my classroom at all.

I'm not there yet. My next goal is to have a few ready-made, action-guiding Core-Sets on my computer. I'll call them «didactic templates» for prompts. I'm a secondary school teacher, and I see real potential in these templates. My time and knowledge are limited — and at the same time I want to deal constructively with the heterogeneity in my class.
I won't just be tweaking a single preset dial on a given task anymore. Not just: harder, easier, more open, more constrained, more support, less support. I know that approach. What's new for me is that I no longer see the dials in isolation. I can deliberately connect several didactic levels: content, representation, action, social dimension, product, creativity, digitality. The result isn't simply a better task — it's a small didactic template. That's the difference: before, I adapted an existing task. Now I first design a didactic blend, and then have suitable tasks, variations, or assessments built from it. AI doesn't replace my decision. But it helps me make my decisions more visible and repeatable. And if I can see more clearly which dials I'm actually turning, I can make more deliberate choices for my students.

For a brief moment I think about the other side — what else could I do with templates like these? A template as a control and monitoring tool in the classroom? I push the thought aside and move on.

After several rounds I define the «Core-Set» as a selection of Didactic Levels, each serving a pedagogical function. GPT suggests «structure», «activate», «differentiate», «reflect», and more. I decide I'll put together three to four of these sets. That will be didactic prompting with AI.

Then I build my very first Core-Set. I call it the «Exploratory Learning Set». Why? Because I spontaneously flash back to the early 2000s and the era of «active-discovery learning» and «Mathe 2000». Good feelings attached. So I say: «Let's give it a try!»

The result feels raw and unfinished in several places — but that's fine. I build another one: a «Competency-Oriented Learning Set», and drop a few other fancy sets again. None of these are new terms, but I want to see which ones actually work as practical handles. With these Core-Sets I could not only create didactically grounded tasks, but also go the other direction — checking whether existing tasks are a good didactic fit. GPT generates, I review didactically and adjust.

Hours have passed since the Major Tom moment. I look back at that instant when I thought: «I like.» And I still think: «Thanks for the nudge!»

09

Matrix: Levels × Core-Sets

A few days later I pick up where I left off, working with the two organizing principles: the didactic levels from the Local List and the Core-Sets.

I have an overview created — a mapping of the levels to the four Core-Sets.

I start wondering what actually happens when GPT assembles these sets and what that matrix looks like. What if I say: "I don't just want an Exploratory Learning Set — I want one that's woven together with the 'Collaboration' or 'Digital Literacy' set." I'm playing with the question of what's possible and what actually makes sense. Is it just decorative layering, or does mixing sets open another door to the future?

I haven't forgotten YAML. It's my didactic prompt key — and of course I put GPT to work on it:

You:
You: Go ahead and build me a YAML file based on this matrix — and feel free to include the adjustment parameters as well.

I let everything flow into it: the didactic levels, the clusters, the core sets, and the adjustment parameters — as a personal didactic meta-tool.

CODE:
Code: version: "1.0" generated: "2025-10-21" context: "Didactic Meta-Levels × Core-Sets (compact 8 levels)" core_sets: - id: explorativ label: "Exploratory – Discover & Understand" - id: kollaborativ label: "Collaborative – Teamwork & Participation" - id: kompetenz label: "Competency-Oriented – Skills & Application" - id: future_digital label: "Future & Digital Literacy – Digitality, Data & Ethics" meta_levels: - id: "C1C2_1-7-8" name: "Content & Thinking" clusters: ["C1", "C2"] levels: - { no: 1, name: "Content Level" } - { no: 7, name: "Cognitive Level" } And so on…

And this YAML looks different. This is no longer the 20-line pseudo-YAML from August — it's a file of 778 words and around 200 lines. Something like this doesn't happen in a few minutes. It takes several evenings, because this file is now part of something bigger taking shape.

Next week I want to test how I can actually translate this Core-Set into a classroom task in History. "Explorers and Conquerors" gives me a possible testing ground — one where students don't just read a source, but where I could deliberately vary that same source through context, perspective, product type, or social form. The task itself won't be the main thing. The real question is whether I can build these sets reliably enough to turn them into actual lessons.

And that's exactly where it gets stuck: something else comes up and gets in the way.
