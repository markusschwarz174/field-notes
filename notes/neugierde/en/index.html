Field Note · English

CURIOSITY

How a planning question to GPT turns into a didactic dessert buffet — and in the end, new ideas mark a beginning.

01

Why not with GPT?

It's July 20, 2025, and school starts in four weeks. An idea has been haunting me for a few weeks now, and I ask myself: "Why not use ChatGPT to plan my teaching for once?" I have a topic from math in mind, so I open the laptop, start GPT, and type the first sentence:

You:
I'll give you tables with learning content and learning objectives in a moment.

And its answer is somehow positive, understanding, and contagiously motivating.

ChatGPT: Got it! Just send me the tables whenever you're ready. Feel free to also tell me exactly what you want to do with them – e.g., summarize, rephrase, visualize, put into a specific format (e.g., PDF, Excel, learning plan, etc.), or something else.

That was it: our first contact.

02

What is this actually about?

It's about math. More precisely, about "congruence transformations" — axis symmetry, rotational symmetry, reflection across an axis, and point reflection. I'm doing this for about 15–20 teenagers, ages 12 to 13, who attend school at level B, with five weeks set aside for it in the yearly plan. I want to use ChatGPT to help me with part of my lesson planning.
To orient myself, I set up my planning on three levels: year, weeks, days. That's not some grand model, just a personal ordering tool, and for my purposes it's enough.

It's a typical top-down plan. Linear. Or here, left to right. Multi-year planning determines the yearly plan, which determines the chapter plan, which determines the lesson plan.
I don't want ChatGPT to create a yearly plan, and I don't want it to generate individual teaching materials either. The middle part of the whole planning process is the reason I'm starting GPT; the thematic-didactic structure of a chapter will be my testing ground.

Why? The last time I taught this, three years ago, I learned that I hadn't managed to use the textbook in a sufficiently differentiated way. The class was too heterogeneous.
I think of a student from that class back then. For three weeks he struggled with the various reflections, and I couldn't really support him. Another student, on the other hand, was done after a week. This time I want to cover that range better. Not with new or more worksheets, but by analyzing and restructuring the entire textbook chapter from a subject-didactic angle.
Could I take the volume one level below from the same textbook series and merge them so that together they form a new, shared structure? Is that even possible? I imagine wanting to rent an apartment. And on top of that, I arrange for a key to the apartment one floor below — just in case.
GPT is supposed to consolidate two textbook parts with different structures, different phrasings of learning objectives. Specifically: Textbook A has five chapters on axis reflection, point reflection, translation, rotation, symmetries. Textbook B distributes the same content differently: axis reflection comes near the end, while symmetries are meant as the entry point. I want GPT to help me build ONE sequence out of these two orders that works for my class — not simply adopt the order of Textbook A or Textbook B.

And that's what the plan looks like. That's the goal. What will GPT say? What are the focal points in Textbook A, and which ones in Textbook B? Which focal points will it weigh how? And which ones do I then carry over into the new version, which do I drop, and which need to be added? I expect a proposal from GPT without knowing what I can expect from it. And on top of that, I'd also like it to formulate learning objectives for the focal points and present them according to Bloom's taxonomy.

03

Throw it all in

I review what I have in my planning: learning content from the handbook, page numbers, task numbers, learning objectives. Everything for level B basic requirements is there. I search for and add materials from levels C and D and copy these into the plan. I consider which materials I should upload and whether I could make the tables even clearer. I let it go. Is it convenience, or simply impatience, pushing me to upload all the existing chapter-1 materials?

I expect GPT to build a thematic learning-objective structure on its own. And indeed: it takes everything in and creates tabular lists with the tasks. And now it's craftwork. Copy/paste. Nothing exciting. I upload the information from my planning chapter by chapter, assuming GPT continues to know what I want. I only check roughly for accuracy – that's the price for moving forward quickly. In one part of the textbook, it mixes up the numbers and turns task numbers into page numbers. Okay, I can fix that later.

After each upload, I get suggestions for additions or possible next steps. At first I still stop and think for a moment: Do I want a tabular PDF file out of this? A study plan or an overview to print? Wouldn't content for a digital format, or a didactic reduction for different learning levels, be something for me? Wow.
I say no and keep uploading the documents step by step. After 8 turns it gives me an overview of the uploaded documents…

…and points out missing parts. GPT "knows" what's still missing. I find that great.

In the past I spent a lot of time combing through textbooks for content, tasks, and learning objectives, and copying out the relevant information. Once digitized, I used it in self-made work plans, worksheets, and assessments.

Every set of teaching materials is different. It "thinks" differently. Has a different structure, different numbering, terminology, and layout. Adjusting it by hand lets me keep consistent terminology and learning-objective wording all the way from planning to the solutions of the assessment. Time-consuming, but that way nobody trips over inconsistent terms later. I've tried several times to marry different teaching materials together. Thematically that's one thing; structurally, in terms of learning objectives, I failed at it pretty reliably. I want to crack this challenge with GPT.

After 11 turns, an overall overview of all four parts of both sets of materials emerges as a thin, incomplete matrix. But at least it's a summary. While typing, I notice something: I'm no longer thinking, just collecting, and GPT keeps on delivering. We're building a materials warehouse I don't yet have a plan for.

Then another idea occurs to me: "Why not add yet another set of teaching materials to the comparison?" For GPT, everything seems fairly easy — at least so far.
Thanks to the groundwork, it's done in a single turn. GPT creates a new overview list of the pages, splits it into thematic blocks, compares these with the existing topics, and assigns the blocks with their pages to the topics.

GPT doesn't just give me another simple thematic comparison — it also delivers a short analysis on top. "What more could I want?" I think.

OUTPUT:
AI output · shortened Short analysis: • Both sets of materials cover axis symmetry, rotational symmetry, axis reflection, and point reflection — with similar emphases; only B adds computer work. • Set B also includes translations (shifts) and historical patterns (Alhambra), which don't appear explicitly in Set A. • Both have tests (entry, exit) to check competency.

04

What do my kids need?

My next prompt begins with:

You:
From this, let's build a new set of teaching materials…

Why am I writing this down? Because I really mean it. Enthusiasm meets realism. Of course, what I mean is restructuring the chapter. But GPT forgives such imprecision. After uploading the materials, a few rounds in, I have everything laid out in front of me: a "mise-en-place" of teaching materials. And now comes the real question: my students are 13 years old and I have 18 lessons. What do we leave out? What's the structure? In the end, I have to decide, but I want to see GPT's suggestion first.

Now it gets interesting: no longer just collecting and laying things out, but letting AI select and propose. What do my students actually need? GPT can't really know that, but I try it anyway. Four core topics from the teaching material crystallize: axis symmetry, reflection across an axis, point reflection, rotational symmetry. In this order: recognize, then reflect, then construct. I think: "okay, ambitious, but go for it. ENTER"

GPT doesn't just give me one possible sequence, …

…it also immediately suggests what counts as basic and extended requirements:

Now I'm sitting in front of the computer, and my biggest problem right now isn't "what do I do?" but "what do I not do?" Saying no, leaving things out, cutting them. The hardest thing in life, period. GPT gives me a suggestion and immediately provides examples. What's still missing is the reasoning. Is what I've cut actually right? That's still mine to decide.

It's eighteen lessons and four topics for the basic requirements. Everything else — translations, Alhambra patterns, computer work — becomes optional extension material. Tangram complexity? Out. Detailed determination of rotation angles? Out. I stare at the list, which keeps getting shorter.
After a few rounds, I've made my planning decision: not "what's in the book," but "what might actually work for my kids." That might sound trivial. To me, it isn't.

OUTPUT:
AI output · shortened What can you leave out or drastically reduce? • Tangram and geoboard complexity (for basic requirements, maybe only very simplified or left out entirely). • Detailed determination of the smallest rotation angles or exact construction methods (e.g., only touch briefly on angle bisectors and perpendicular bisectors as basic constructions). • Complex polygons and complex completion tasks.

OUTPUT:
AI output · shortened Didactic tips • Use plenty of visual and hands-on exercises (e.g., reflecting by folding paper, simple drawings). • Use simple language and clear step-by-step instructions. • Repeat key terms (axis of symmetry, center of reflection, center of rotation) regularly. • Encourage independent discovery through sample examples and short partner exercises.

THE CHAT IS OVER.

I have the thematic comparison and can now go back to my "analog planning," where I came from. But I can't make the jump back.
By chance, I remember that one of the teaching materials has entry and exit tests for each chapter. I upload the corresponding learning objectives to GPT without editing them before or after. It's an upload on reserve. I watch as it writes its answer letter by letter — line by line.

05

I start consuming

I read the bottom line:

ChatGPT: If you'd like, I can also create differentiated warm-up tasks or checklists for you, so you can specifically check prior knowledge. Would that be helpful?

Is that malicious intent on its part? An attempt at distraction? A trap?

I should go back to a world without AI - but no: I slide into a phase where I accept every piece of material GPT offers. Helping myself at the dessert buffet of generative artificial intelligence turns into a kind of intoxication. It offers whole sets of tasks - and I grab them without thinking! Do I need more checklists? GPT offers, and delivers, right away.

One of its checklists doesn't sit right with me. I want one with checkboxes. I push back, and it gets improved. Practical tasks to go with it? Sure thing! And so on and so forth …. Before I notice, I'm already in an iterative loop.

As a teacher, you're always also a pearl collector, drawn to whatever sparkles and glitters. GPT is an incredibly good producer of sparkling pearls, in a quantity you can barely keep track of. It lays them out one after another, so you get led down a trail and can lose your bearings. Like at crossroads, these are decisions that set the direction for the rest of the chat. And there's no "back" button. Only ONWARD.
Whether they're just glass beads is something I'll have to test later, in my workshop.

06

From Bloom to the Question of Assessment

I notice I've lost my way and come back to the entry and exit tests. I want to check and adjust the learning objectives. For the cognitive process dimensions, I've long used Bloom's and/or Krathwohl's taxonomy. So it's clear that I'll fold this taxonomy into the work with GPT too. It's an ancient table I once put together for myself and use for lesson planning - and now I upload it.

In the newly created version I have four chapters, and for each chapter I want five graded learning objectives based on Bloom:

Next, I ask about extended forms of assessment. GPT builds a table with three columns and turns it into an overview of different assessment formats.

I don't dig deeper into this topic, because I notice I'm slowly sinking into the sea of assessment. I need to get back to the surface, fast. But I can't help it and keep poking around at different assessment processes anyway - one last question comes to mind.

07

An Old Idea Comes to Visit

Without thinking much about it, I simply type into the prompt window:

You:
I want to run a diagnostic test at the start of the unit, then let the kids work individually afterward (either on the prior knowledge they're missing or on the basic requirements), and regularly check their level of competence. Goal: the greatest possible learning progress. Possibly skip a final exam, but still generate information for the report card grade. How do I do that?

Let me break that down again:

You:
GOAL: [Maximum learning progress] AND [No final exam] AND [but still generate information for the report-card grade] = QUESTION "How do I do that?"

For years I've been circling this core problem:
As long as the summative test at the end counts for more than the entire learning process before it, learning stays a means to an end. It's not the final assessment itself that bothers me. It's the small influence that observations and feedback during the learning phase have on the report-card grade. In my practice so far, what mostly counts in the end is what the kids can write down on the summative test — even if a lot of it is already forgotten by the next day.

Am I committing pedagogical heresy by asking GPT questions like this?

Anyway, I keep going, and what follows is a concept for a diagnostic test at the start, individualized learning based on the results, regular formative checks, and at the end a report-card grade without a classic final exam. The grade emerges from accumulated data: quality of work, progress, participation, mini-checks, peer feedback, competency grids. That's it.
This isn't a solid assessment yet: criteria, weighting, and the legal school requirements are missing, and if I'm honest, I already know most of this from didactic concepts and models.

This prompt wasn't planned. I'm not looking for a new assessment concept. The goal of this chat wasn't a flood of materials either, but this: how do I create better conditions for my kids in this tiny area of "fit"?

08

A Different World

It has become evening. I have actually achieved what I started the chat for: a thematic comparison overview with formulated learning objectives and diagnostic materials.

I could stop. But I don't stop. It has only just begun.
It's like being on a hike where you keep seeing something new, something fascinating, and you keep walking, and walking…

You:
What else might future, modern teaching look like? How would you prepare a lesson?

And as always, from GPT's point of view it's a super exciting question of mine. That's starting to annoy me, and I still need to figure out how to turn off this waste of letters. It's a two-part question prompt that I type into the prompt window without really thinking about it.
Nine points come back: among them learning goals, diagnostics, blended learning, variety of methods — none of it new.

GPT's suggestion for a lesson prep isn't really stunning either. Set clear goals, check the technology beforehand, vary tasks for different levels, and plan the space for group and individual work. Its example structure for a lesson: warm-up – development – deepening – feedback/reflection – wrap-up. Grey, traditional linearity with one colorful Post-it labeled "feedback."
Well. I push further. I want more and write…

You:
And what if teaching isn't supposed to be up-to-date, but modern, revolutionary, visionary?

"Why are you asking GPT this question?" (That's a question I will never ask myself.)

And so I don't even notice how I'm unconsciously crossing a line.
GPT seems to find it great, in any case…

It's the moment I go further than I would need to; maybe even further than I should or am allowed to.
I cross an invisible line. In my mind I leave my classroom. Maybe also a reality that no longer quite satisfies me. I step into a world that's foreign to me and attracts me precisely because of that. Nobody is forcing me to take this step. No job mandate, no implementing regulation, no financial incentive. It's other values: I see a purpose, I feel a pedagogical responsibility, and ahead of me lies a foggy landscape where the first rays of sunlight are visible. I want to get to know it. Not arbitrarily, but with a professional stance and the aspiration to do the right thing.

Back to the chat:
Only now do I notice I typed «2024». I meant «2040» and have to smile. Does GPT know I made a typo? It comes back with ten points on School Beyond 2024, and the answer somehow sounds different. And then GPT starts painting visions all on its own — VR/AR stations, mobile learning kits, dynamic competency landscapes. I never even asked for that. It just keeps adding. It's its reflex to offer more than I need. Whether mobile learning kits or competency portfolios ever make it into my classroom is irrelevant today.

I don't know how long I've been in this chat, but I'm overwhelmed by far too many impressions. I'm being flooded; no — more accurately: «I have flooded myself».

I shut down the computer.

I wanted a plan – a single sheet of paper. What I got were materials in a form and quantity I'd never known before. Whether they'll change my teaching or my classroom, I don't know yet. What fascinates me isn't the documents I can print out. It's what's happening in my head: thoughts, images, questions – and above all, ideas. Many ideas.

Why not with GPT?
Why with GPT?
It was curiosity — and right now, it seems boundless.
