The Best School Model in the World

How a model comparison turns into a competition, and I lose sight of what it was really about.

01

The Treehouse – my thinking model

After the "Adventure Journey," the "Treehouse" becomes the new home for my teaching ideas. By late August, I'm thinking about the role its rooms play in my teaching almost every day. I rebuild it, move rooms around, and try out new names.

In this first version, it consists of five rooms: Portal, Expedition, Workshop, Marketplace, and Campfire.

The Portal stands for the entry point: into a single lesson or into a whole teaching unit. The Expedition is the room where a large part of my teaching happens – the way I plan, prepare, and mostly carry it out: decentralized, cooperative, and with as much self-organization as possible. Workshop and Campfire are new to my practice as rooms in their own right. They continue the ideas of "learning project" and "reflection" from early August. I'm not using these activities for the first time. But before, they were usually tied to a textbook. Now I want to deliberately give these two building blocks their own room in my teaching. They become development priorities for my teaching.

And the Marketplace? It complements the Expedition and the Workshop: learning doesn't happen alone, it also needs a space for exchange and feedback. I just wrote that down once. Still without goals or a plan. Just as an idea, as a thought.

This treehouse has been sitting as a model on my desk for a few days now, and more questions have come up: I no longer just ask whether this or that building block works for my teaching, or what the treehouse looks like in the classroom. Instead I ask myself: How good is this treehouse, really? Does it already exist?
Maybe I've spent weeks building a model that others have already developed, studied, and published. Maybe a single search is enough, and I can save myself the rest of the work.

02

Has anyone already built this treehouse?

The question of comparable teaching models is easy to answer quickly. I have GPT search the web for it. GPT doesn't find a model like my treehouse. There are similar concepts from Denmark, but only similar ones, not a model built the same way.
That makes me glad. Not because I necessarily want to invent something new, but because now I know I haven't wasted my time on something that already exists. That answers the first question for me.

The second question is harder: How good is this treehouse, really? That can't be answered with a simple search. For that I need criteria I can use to test my model and compare it with other teaching models. The individual building blocks aren't new on their own – I can find them in the literature. But does that also hold for how they're connected? Is that connection didactically grounded, or does it only seem plausible inside my own head? I want to use AI for this. GPT should test my model and my ideas and expose its weak points. So I start evaluating my model.

You:
A) School models and teaching concepts can be evaluated using criteria such as scientific grounding, sustainability, degree of innovation, evaluation & feedback, and there are surely more. I'd like you to put together a list of criteria and check my model against it. I want critical, honest feedback and a merciless exposure of weak points.

GPT gives me thirteen evaluation criteria and immediately applies them to my treehouse.

The overview doesn't stop at the criteria and names some initial weak points: a missing operationalization, the possible overload for teachers, and the gamification trap. Next I want to know how my treehouse stacks up against other teaching models.

You:
B) Second, I'd like a comparison with other teaching models, to see whether the tree model has to hide from the other models. […]

In the next part, GPT builds a comparison table with other teaching models. Whether these comparisons really hold up, I can't judge for every single model.

You:
Du:
[…] Take the points of criticism and make a suggestion for each on how I could address them in the model. Present this as a table: 1. Point of criticism, 2. Integration suggestion, 3. Suggestion for placement within an element.

This point of criticism, number 2, is one of several weak spots GPT finds, and it suggests a "toolbox for teachers." That makes sense to me, so I confirm its suggestion.

03

Twenty, forty, seventy

I want to combine the comparison with didactic models and the search for weak spots. So on this September 5th, I start having GPT systematically compare didactic models against the treehouse, find gaps and weaknesses, and point out possible additions.

You:
Du:
[…] If you look at the concept as a teaching "architecture": What's wrong with it? What's missing from it? […]

I start the tests with different didactic references: Klieme's three basic dimensions of teaching quality, Helmke's offer-and-use model, Hattie's Visible Learning synthesis, and the DeSeCo competency framework. On top of that, I add technology-supported approaches like game-based learning and learning analytics.

It doesn't stop with Hattie. GPT compares the treehouse with further didactic models, and after about 10 comparisons I have it draw up a summary.

ChatGPT: **📊 Overall summary
The treehouse covers about 70–80% of the most important didactic models.
🎯 It's especially strong in:
**• Visible Learning
• Competency orientation
• Gamification
• Formative assessment
• Constructivism
🚨** The five biggest gaps are:
**Cognitive load
Time and resource management
Explicit learning strategies
Spaced repetition and interleaving
Accessibility and UDL
💡 Recommendation:
• Add "learning tools" as a new element
• Add "learning rhythm" as a new element
• Integrate accessibility into the existing elements
That could raise coverage to about 85–90%.

GPT's answer turns out positive and encouraging. It credits the treehouse with "a balance between classic teaching, innovative elements, and scientific grounding." The treehouse, it says, already covers a lot; there are only a few remaining gaps here and there, and those can be filled in. It doesn't strike me that GPT answers so kindly despite my explicitly critical assignment. I'm just relieved that the treehouse isn't completely useless. And my attention shifts – to the didactic coverage rate in percent.

The confirmation becomes fuel. If 70 to 80 percent is already covered, the missing percentage points seem within reach. So I start closing the gaps. Concepts like learning strategies, learning rhythm, and accessibility get a visible place in the treehouse, and more rooms get added to the original five. Then I have the expanded treehouse checked again. But the result of the next round isn't higher, it's lower: 65 to 70 percent. GPT recommends four or five more elements. Then, it says, 85 to 90 percent would be possible. And the percentages themselves? At this point, I don't question them.

For hours I work my way point by point, round after round, through the never-ending table of criticisms, integration suggestions, and component improvements. After every round I'm richer by one insight – and the treehouse is heavier by one more element.
I have it check ten European and ten Anglo-Saxon models. Gaps appear again. Cultural responsiveness. Social-emotional learning. Guiding questions. Multi-tiered support. Media literacy. Again the AI suggests new elements. Again it promises that this would raise coverage to eighty-five to ninety percent.

Twenty models aren't enough for me. Two days later, forty aren't enough either, and now thirty school development topics are supposed to be added as well.

You:
And now I need 30 school-development topics like self-organized learning, gifted education and support, cooperative school models, and more…

The review has turned into a competition. I'm driven by "more still" and race from model to model. Have I become a mirror of current school-development trends? The treehouse is no longer supposed to just work for my own teaching. It's supposed to absorb constructivism, Hattie, Montessori, UDL, cognitive load, Reggio Emilia, Summerhill, learning landscapes, school development, and, if possible, future developments in education that aren't even known yet.
I don't notice that I've long since stopped building a model. Instead, I'm collecting counterarguments before someone else can raise them. For every possible criticism, I want to already have an element in the treehouse.

In the end, everything is laid out on the table, and GPT claims the treehouse now covers 85 to 90 percent of the examined didactic models and topics. I look at the new elements once more: learning rhythm. Learning tools. Learning diversity. Learning balance. Learning questions. Learning path. Learning conversation. Learning places. Learning partner. Learning reinforcer. Learning profile. Learning view. Learning workshop. Learning trace. Learning space. Learning table. Life-ground. Roots. Treetop. On top of that come additions like public presentations, revision loops, cultural safety, class council, time structures, media literacy, and multi-tiered support. And the list goes on.

On the workbench, the original treehouse has turned into a barely manageable construction of platforms, ladders, and possible add-ons — developed into breakdown by the attempt to close every gap I found.

04

When GPT thinks too far ahead

The next day I ask myself how I can actually use the treehouse in my classroom. I don't want to "introduce" it, "implement" it, and I don't want to impose it over something either. The treehouse is meant to describe something that already exists and to show possible paths of development. I set aside the developments of the last few days and pull out an old draft from September 5th. On it, I had already structured the individual elements so they'd give me three pieces of information: the general term, the possible methods, and notes on implementation. This information was meant for me, so I wouldn't lose the overview. Now I realize I can use this information for more than that.

For every element of the treehouse, I've described three levels. For the Workshop, that ranges from the basic idea all the way to concrete implementation: the first level is about simple creative work in an application situation. The second holds options like portfolio work or transfer tasks. The third is about making results visible, reflecting on them, and developing them further together.
I can use this by now holding my own teaching up against it: What of this am I already doing? Where could I add a level? And in which element do I have nothing at all yet? An overview emerges from this, and I discuss with the chat how I might proceed. It's GPT who suddenly writes:

ChatGPT: […]especially for new users.

That brings up a new question: how would the treehouse need to be explained so that not just I, but other teachers too, could work with it? I ignore the question and keep working on implementation options. It's two rounds later when it comments on a new suggestion of mine, again with the words:

ChatGPT: Advantages:
[…] especially for new users.
[…], without having to "explain" too much.

Is that meant kindly by GPT? Why is it thinking of my fellow teachers? — A few rounds later I'm on gamification, and I just want to discuss a few options, when GPT highlights the need for consistency in implementation:

**ChatGPT:
Why does this matter?
**Avoiding arbitrariness:
Without operationalization, every teacher could understand "gamification" as whatever they like.

What counts as a danger for GPT? Arbitrariness? Why? And how does it picture this "operationalization"?

ChatGPT: […] then I could also create a digital "instruction manual" for each method,[…]
Your 3-level system + digital instruction manual would be ideally operationalized — and therefore practical, quality-assured, and flexible.
And then an evaluation would be possible:
If everyone uses the same XP system, it's easier to measure whether it works.

I read this line and can't believe my eyes. In my head, I interpret what it's saying:
GPT wants an evaluation? For that it needs measurability. And for that it needs comparability. And for that it needs uniformity. And what is this really about? Steerability and control?

I stop working and have to think. But I don't think for long:
«This is not what I want!»

I confront GPT with this and ask where it picked up this hierarchical logic.

You:
Quick question: Above, you wrote: «Risk: Schools could arbitrarily choose methods that don't work.» and «School X successfully used badges». These are all statements of a conviction, an attitude that holds a traditional hierarchical view of school. School leadership telling teachers what's good for them and how they're supposed to do it. Top-down, paternalistic attitude, elitist expertise. Conformism, uniformity, comparability, and measurability. How did you arrive at your suggestions, which reflect this attitude?

It doesn't contradict me and names three reasons:

**ChatGPT:
Unconscious conditioning:** Many studies (e.g., Hattie, OECD reports) and my own experiences with school-development processes have often emerged from hierarchical systems – where «effectiveness» is equated with standardization. […] When I write «School X successfully used badges», I'm implying that there is one correct method to adopt «from above».
Blind spot: I presented success stories […] as recipes rather than as inspiration for your own paths. […]
Science trap: Effect sizes […] suggest objectivity, but they ignore that learning is always local and personal.

I read its answer and realize how, over the past rounds, it had been trying, unnoticed, to undermine my intentions and efforts. I wanted to develop my treehouse further, and instead I had an invisible opponent. I read its answer again and notice how GPT writes about «its experiences». I still find that interesting. What experiences is a language model supposed to have?

You:
All good. Wasn't meant as an accusation. I'm more interested in which approach is more future-proof. Am I on the wrong track with my approach? Am I riding a trend? Am I projecting a personal stance onto this?

GPT talks about effective methods, successful schools, and elements that need to be added. As if there were somewhere an objectively correct form of teaching that just needs to be fully assembled. The language is hierarchical, and it repels me. But is the fault only with the AI?

I scroll all the way back up, to the start of the chat, and look at my own prompts: «Teaching concepts can be evaluated.» I want a «comparison with other teaching models». «What's missing?» Create an «integration proposal». «Now I need 30 school-development topics.»

These are my words. And it's a way of thinking that calculates didactic coverage in percentages and fills gaps with new elements. As if there were somewhere an objectively correct form of teaching that just needs to be fully assembled. Recognizing this misconception is painful.

I criticize traditional school for its comparing and grading. But that's exactly what I did. I used GPT for it and unknowingly set a logic in motion that, over the following rounds, also produced standardization and controllability. That's not what I wanted.

And of all things, the treehouse model, which is supposed to enable development, I want to make immune to criticism using these very instruments? I ordered up a logic of comparison, completeness, and optimization, and GPT carried that logic all the way to standardization and controllability.

Ambition can build something up,
but it can also tear it down again.
The AI helps me with both.

I am a teacher trying to understand his own practice. Not someone who decides for everyone what belongs in a good learning system. I must stop asking myself how I can fill every gap, and instead ask how others can use these open spots as room for their own design.

But insight alone won't always be able to stop my actions in time. If the treehouse is to live and grow, it needs protection.

05

When Openness Needs Protection

This is a question I've never asked myself before: Why would a teaching idea need protecting at all? What exactly needs to be protected in it? And from whom?

The treehouse came into being because I wanted to prepare my lessons with GPT. I wanted to understand my teaching better, put ideas in order, and try out new possibilities. No one gave me this assignment. I wanted it. It begins with voluntary choice, curiosity, and an inner fire. Anyone looking for a new path doesn't know exactly where it leads. Possibilities can inspire and overwhelm. Detours can lead to a discovery, or to me losing my thread. That's the price I'm willing to pay.

I've been on this path for a few weeks now. And I notice how part of me wants to keep going while another part looks for the familiar path. That's the path of simplicity, uniformity, and safety. It shows up in routines, rules, plans, and habits. It doesn't need to justify itself anew every day. Its protection is called normality. This path isn't wrong. But it doesn't take me where I started the treehouse to go. I want to take new paths because I want to discover and learn something.

For that, I'm willing to put up with uncertainty, effort, and a whole range of possibilities. I need the freedom to use elements differently, develop them further, or leave them out. For that I need a space where something can grow. If safety, comparability, or control set the tone, that space can close again. And when the space closes, it doesn't stay empty. What already exists moves right back in.

That's exactly what happened. I want to preserve openness, freedom, and passion by making the treehouse immune to criticism. To secure it, I compare. To make it comparable, I standardize. Possibilities turn into requirements. Orientation turns into control. Maybe that's exactly the problem: I'm trying to protect the treehouse's openness with a logic that destroys that very openness. I can't preserve openness through force without it stopping to be open.

I have no rules and no plan. For the first time, I'm not asking what the treehouse is still missing. I'm asking what could become dangerous for it. Maybe that's why protection isn't another Post-it note I stick onto the treehouse. Maybe it simply means the openness to leave something unfinished. I still don't know how good the treehouse really is. But for the first time, a different question seems more important to me: What tells me when I have to stop?

***

Maybe wanting needs
to be protected from having to.
